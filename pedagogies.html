Pedagogies

Teaching and assessment methods you can use on Monday. Each one says what it is, why it works, and where it goes wrong.

Please note. These are suggestions and directions for exploration, not settled answers. They draw on published research and practice but have not been peer reviewed. Adapt what you use to your context, and check your institution’s policies before acting on it.

Design the task

Moves that make a task worth doing well, and make AI-only completion obviously inadequate.

  • Secure the anchor

    Attach a short secured element, such as a viva or in-class component, to an otherwise open task.

    Why it works. It separates assurance from development (Dawson, 2021; Bridgeman, Liu and Weeks, 2024).

    Watch for. Untrained markers make unreliable oral judgements. Invest in rubrics and calibration.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • Localise

    Ground the task in data, texts, places or events specific to this cohort and moment: this term’s seminar disagreement, a dataset the class collected.

    Why it works. Authenticity (Gulikers, Bastiaens and Kirschner, 2004), and it removes much of the model’s training-data advantage.

    Watch for. Novelty without cognitive demand isn’t rigour.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • Personalise to experience

    Make the work accountable to the student’s own placement, fieldwork, practice or observed encounter.

    Why it works. Situated learning (Lave and Wenger, 1991).

    Watch for. Privacy and equity: not every student has equal access to sites or experiences.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • Assess the question, not the answer

    Have students formulate the research question, design the study, write the brief or specify the problem.

    Why it works. Finding problems is higher-order than solving them, and models support it less well.

    Watch for. Question formulation has to be taught; it can’t be assumed.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • Assess the critique

    Give students an AI-generated or deliberately flawed artefact and assess their diagnosis, correction and justification.

    Why it works. It builds evaluative judgement directly (Tai et al., 2018; Bearman et al., 2024).

    Watch for. If the flaws are obvious it becomes an error hunt. Seed subtle, discipline-specific errors.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • Assess the transformation

    Students turn work from one mode into another for a different audience: a report into a public explainer, a case file into a client conversation.

    Why it works. It tests transfer and audience awareness.

    Watch for. AI shifts register well, so pair it with an accountability element.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • Add contingency

    Make later stages depend on earlier individual choices, with staged submission and feedback between stages.

    Why it works. It spreads effort across the task (Gibbs and Simpson, 2004) and makes wholesale delegation costly.

    Watch for. Staff workload. Use light checkpoints and peer processes.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • Add an audience

    Real clients, community partners, exhibitions, published outputs or cross-cohort peer review.

    Why it works. Authenticity and accountability, and it motivates.

    Watch for. External partners need ethical and reputational care.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • Make it collaborative and observed

    Group work with individually assessed contributions, observed interaction or role-differentiated tasks.

    Why it works. The social dimension of authenticity (Gulikers, Bastiaens and Kirschner, 2004).

    Watch for. Free-riding. Always pair with individual attribution.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • Embody it

    Assessment that needs physical presence and performance: lab technique, clinical examination, fieldwork, making, teaching.

    Why it works. Inherently secure, and it assesses what text never could.

    Watch for. Resource-intensive, and accessibility needs designing in.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • Extend the timescale

    Longitudinal portfolios and programme-level projects assessed for development over time, not quality at a moment.

    Why it works. Programmatic and sustainable assessment (van der Vleuten et al., 2012; Boud and Falchikov, 2006).

    Watch for. Admin overhead and late cramming unless checkpointed.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • Assess the use of AI itself

    The assessed object becomes how the student uses AI: their prompts, how they verify, when they override, and their reasoning about disclosure and bias.

    Why it works. Critical AI literacy (Roe, Furze and Perkins, 2026; Bearman and Ajjawi, 2023).

    Watch for. It dates fast and can become tool training. Assess the reasoning, not the interface.

    From: Assessment redesign map

Make the process visible

Where the process is the learning, the process has to be assessable. Lightest first.

  • The reasoning note

    200–400 words with any open task: what did you try, what did you reject, what’s still uncertain, where did you get stuck?

    Why it works. Cheap and revealing, and hard to fake because it needs knowledge of a particular struggle.

    Watch for. Any process evidence can be fabricated. Use it for its learning value, not as surveillance.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • The decision log

    Dated entries recording choices and their justifications across a project.

    Why it works. Makes judgement visible as it happens, not reconstructed afterwards.

    Watch for. It adds load, heaviest on students managing disability, caring or paid work.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • Version history

    Submit the document or commit history alongside the work.

    Why it works. Natural in computing, and increasingly available in document editors.

    Watch for. It shows drafting, not thinking, and it can be gamed.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • The annotated AI transcript

    Students submit their AI interaction with the work, annotated where the model was wrong, where they overrode it, and why.

    Why it works. It assesses critical AI literacy directly, and is hard to do well without doing the work.

    Watch for. Treat it as a taught genre, not an added burden.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • The patchwork text

    Small, varied, low-stakes pieces across a module, stitched together at the end with an integrating commentary.

    Why it works. Spreads effort, disrupts last-minute delegation, and evidences development.

    Watch for. Needs checkpoints so the pieces really are produced along the way.

    From: Assessment redesign map

Secure the evidence

Formats for the few points where you need individual, attributable evidence. Best value per hour first.

  • Interactive oral assessment

    An unscripted professional conversation of 10–20 minutes, marked against criteria, with follow-ups that depend on the answers.

    Why it works. It assesses depth, ownership and adaptive reasoning (Sotiriadou et al., 2020), and scales better than its reputation.

    Watch for. Needs trained markers running in parallel, and adjustments for anxious and multilingual students.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • The portfolio viva

    A short oral defence of open, unsecured work.

    Why it works. The highest-leverage design of all: open tasks stay authentic and AI-permissive, and attribution is restored without any detection tool.

    Watch for. A student who can’t discuss their own reasoning hasn’t met the outcome. Make that expectation clear in advance.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • Observed performance

    Clinical, laboratory, studio, field, classroom or courtroom performance.

    Why it works. Naturally secure, and already the backbone of professional programmes.

    Watch for. Resource-heavy; plan accessible alternatives.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • Supervised in-class production

    Writing, coding or problem-solving under supervision, with the access rules stated.

    Why it works. Modest cost, and well suited to securing foundational skills in year one.

    Watch for. Rewards speed under pressure if over-used.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • Structured written exam

    A timed, invigilated exam.

    Why it works. Still valid where the thing being assessed really is unaided recall or reasoning under time pressure.

    Watch for. The failure isn’t exams; it’s exams as a blanket substitute for design.

    From: Assessment redesign map

Feedback with AI

Using AI to multiply feedback without letting it near judgement.

  • The interrogating tutor

    Configure AI to question rather than answer: withhold the answer, ask one question at a time, give hints only on evidence of effort, make students explain before moving on.

    Why it works. Unlimited, instant developmental feedback without doing the thinking for the student.

    Watch for. Students will try to game it for answers. Academics should write and version the instructions.

    From: The Threshold Model

  • The feedback response note

    Students document what they changed after feedback, what they rejected, and why.

    Why it works. It evidences uptake, not just receipt, of feedback (Carless and Boud, 2018), and assesses judgement cheaply.

    Watch for. It becomes box-ticking if never read. Mark it lightly but always.

    From: Assessment redesign map

  • Calibrated peer review

    Students mark exemplars against tutor marks before reviewing each other’s work.

    Why it works. Comparison generates internal feedback (Nicol, 2021).

    Watch for. Uncalibrated peers mislead; the calibration step is not optional.

    From: The Threshold Model

Work with text

Using generated text as a comparator to teach how disciplinary writing works.

  • Comparative text analysis

    Put an expert, a student and a generated text side by side on the same topic and genre, and analyse moves, stance, citation and specificity.

    Why it works. Generated text is a fluent but hollow comparator, which makes disciplinary conventions visible (Hyland, 2005).

    Watch for. Activities that depend on a model’s current quirks are disposable. Design against durable properties.

    From: Academic literacies and AI

  • The specificity audit

    Rate every claim in a text from fully specified to unspecified, and compare the ratio across texts.

    Why it works. The single most productive stage of the text-analysis protocol.

    Watch for. Needs disciplinary ground truth to judge what “specified” means.

    From: Academic literacies and AI

  • The disciplinary rewrite

    Students rewrite one generated paragraph until their discipline would accept it.

    Why it works. It shows, in the student’s own words, what the model couldn’t supply.

    Watch for. Keep the student’s own text in the room, not just the model’s.

    From: Academic literacies and AI

AI's role, level by level

The same stance can be right in year three and wrong in year one. Novices can't yet spot a model's errors, so offloading too early stops them building the knowledge they need (Kirschner, Sweller and Clark, 2006; Bjork and Bjork, 2011).

Default stance on AI use at each level of study
Level AI as What that means
Foundation and Year 1TutorExplanation, retrieval practice and worked examples. Restricted as a producer of core work.
Year 2CriticThe student produces, AI critiques, the student decides. Builds judgement while keeping the effort.
Year 3CollaboratorProduction may be shared, but the student is accountable for every claim, and assessment moves to judgement and defence.
MastersInfrastructurePart of research and production, with methods reported transparently.
DoctoralDeclared methodGoverned by research integrity and publisher norms. AI can’t be an author; original contribution can’t be delegated.
