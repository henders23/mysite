Oral assessment and vivas at scale

How to run interactive orals and vivas on submitted work for whole cohorts: formats, a question bank, marking and moderation, an honest costing, fair adjustments and the UK legal floor.

Please note. These are suggestions and directions for exploration, not settled answers. They draw on published research and practice but have not been peer reviewed. Adapt what you use to your context, and check your institution’s policies before acting on it.

Why orals carry the weight

The assessment redesign map on this site secures a few assurance points across a programme and leaves other tasks open. Orals carry much of that weight, because a conversation tests what a file cannot: whether the student can explain, adapt and defend the reasoning in their own work.

The evidence is encouraging but thin. Interactive orals were developed at Griffith University from 2015 (Ward et al., 2024). In a single-institution survey study, Sotiriadou et al. (2020) reported that scaffolded tasks ending in an interactive oral helped deter misconduct and built employability skills. In a Dublin City University pilot, 82% of responding students said the format encouraged good integrity behaviour, but that was self-report from 140 of 322 students, with no comparison group (Ward et al., 2024).

The best controlled evidence predates generative AI. When 99 biology students were randomly assigned to oral or written versions of the same questions, the oral group scored significantly higher, with no evidence that any group was disadvantaged, but felt more nervous (Huxham, Campbell and Westwood, 2012). Treat orals as a well-reasoned design choice, not a proven fix, and evaluate your own.

Three formats, and where to hold them

Choose by what you need to secure. Joughin’s (1998) dimensions make a useful checklist: what the oral tests, how interactive, structured and authentic it is, and who examines.

  • Interactive oralAn unscripted, scenario-based professional conversation of 10–20 minutes. The student briefs a ‘client’ or advises a ‘patient’, and the marker follows up on the answers, guided by the rubric. Best as a standalone assurance point for applied, adaptive reasoning.
  • Viva on submitted work10–15 minutes on the student’s own portfolio, project or dissertation. Decide in advance whether it confirms, adjusts or gates the mark, and put that in the regulations and the brief. Apply it to everyone or to a transparent random sample: selecting on suspicion makes it an investigation.
  • Supervised oral defenceThe student prepares under supervision, say 20 minutes with an unseen case, then defends their analysis to two examiners for 15 minutes. The most secure and most expensive format. Keep it for capstones and professional thresholds.
  • In the room or online?Online orals suit development but are not secure: real-time AI help and synthetic live video are both possible (Kalman, Miller and Liu, 2024). Hold assurance orals in person where you can.

Viva question bank

Publish the purposes, not the questions, so students know what kind of thinking to expect. Pick one or two prompts per purpose, follow the answer rather than the script, and fill the brackets from the student’s work.

VIVA QUESTION BANK – [module code], [task title]

OPEN (one minute, to settle nerves)
– In two sentences, what does your [work] argue or do?

1. EXPLAIN A CHOICE
– Why [method / source / design decision] rather than [alternative]?
– Talk me through how you got from [data or reading] to [claim on page X].
– What did you leave out, and why?

2. EXTEND
– With another [month / dataset], what would you do next, and why that?
– How would your conclusion change if [assumption] did not hold?

3. APPLY TO A NEW CASE
– Here is [short new case or dataset]. Does your argument still hold? Where does it bend?
– How would your approach work for [stakeholder] in [different context]?

4. FIND THE WEAKNESS
– What is the strongest objection to [claim], and your answer to it?
– Which piece of your evidence is weakest? What would strengthen it?
– [Source] argues [counter-position]. Where is it right?

PROCESS (vivas on submitted work)
– How did you work on this: drafts, notes, tools, and any AI use you declared?
– Show me where your thinking changed between draft and final version.

CLOSE
– Anything you want to clarify or add?

FOLLOW-UPS: ‘Tell me more about…’ · ‘Can you give an example?’ · ‘Let me put that another way…’ Rephrasing once in plainer words is not leading.

Marking, recording and calibration

  1. 01Mark the construct, not the performanceName what the oral secures: ownership of reasoning, accuracy, adaptation, handling objections. Leave out fluency, accent and confidence unless spoken communication is an outcome; otherwise they add construct-irrelevant variance (Messick, 1989). Keep to four or five criteria.
  2. 02Mark live, finish in the changeoverNote evidence against each criterion as you go and settle the mark before the next student. Dublin City University plans 10 minutes of conversation plus 5 of marking, and at most about 20 orals per examiner per day (Dublin City University, 2026).
  3. 03Record every assurance oralAudio is usually enough, and it serves moderation, external examiners and appeals. Tell students in the brief, and agree storage and retention with your data protection officer first.
  4. 04Calibrate, then check for driftAll examiners mark the same two or three recorded examples and agree what each band sounds like. Repeat a short check midway: judgements drift with fatigue.
  5. 05Moderate by sample and by riskA second marker reviews recordings across bands and examiners, plus every fail and borderline. Look for patterns by examiner, slot time and first language.
  6. 06Keep assessment and investigation apartIf a misconduct concern arises during an assessment viva, stop and refer it to your misconduct procedure, which has its own rules on evidence, notice and representation.

Cost it honestly

Orals move workload rather than remove it, and they save time only if they replace written marking. The hours also bunch. One Sydney unit ran interactive orals for 690 students over two weeks (Stevenson, Miller and Sitbon, 2025); a Queensland course of 230 needed three weeks of room bookings and found the work time-intensive (Lodge, 2025).

Fairness by design

Build fairness into the format before term, not student by student in the week before.

  • Reduce the anxiety you don’t needStudents feel more nervous about orals (Huxham, Campbell and Westwood, 2012). Run a practice oral, publish the purposes and a recorded example, and frame it as a professional conversation, not a defence (Lodge, 2025). Anxiety that remains may be disability-related and need an adjustment.
  • Design the alternative in advanceFor every assurance oral, prepare an equally rigorous alternative for the same outcome: written questions in advance, answers typed in real time, one examiner rather than a panel, or a different room or time, as the EHRC suggests (Equality and Human Rights Commission, 2024). Designing for diversity beats retrofitting (Tai et al., 2023).
  • Act on what you seeMissed slots, visible distress or a gap between written and oral performance can require action without a diagnosis. Don’t simply pass it to the disability service; agree an escalation route for imminent assessments.
  • Assess substance, not EnglishUnless spoken English is the outcome, allow a one-page glossary, repeated questions and thinking time, and train examiners to rephrase. See ‘Supporting multilingual students fairly’.
  • Allow for communication differencesEye contact, pace, pauses and directness are not evidence of understanding or authorship. Brief examiners, especially for autistic students.

The UK legal frame

A summary for planning, not legal advice. Check the specifics with the people named in the last item.

  • The duty to make reasonable adjustments

    Universities in Great Britain must take reasonable steps to avoid the substantial disadvantage a provision, criterion or practice puts a disabled student at (Equality Act 2010, ss. 20 and 91). The duty is anticipatory: plan for disabled students in general, not only those who have disclosed (Equality and Human Rights Commission, 2024). Northern Ireland has separate legislation.

    Competence standards are exempt: standards applied to determine whether someone has a particular level of competence or ability (Equality Act 2010, sch. 13, para. 4). The method of testing them usually is not.

  • Abrahart: what was decided

    Natasha Abrahart, a Bristol physics student with social anxiety disorder and depression, was required to pass assessed oral laboratory interviews and a laboratory conference. She died in April 2018. In May 2022 Bristol County Court found that the University had failed to make reasonable adjustments, and had discriminated against her both because of something arising from her disability and indirectly.

    In February 2024 the High Court dismissed the University’s appeal (University of Bristol v Abrahart [2024]). The oral format was not a competence standard, because the assessed process did not automatically require face-to-face oral interaction. Removing the oral element or giving written questions in advance were reasonable adjustments, and failing to make them was not justified. The duty did not depend on a diagnosis; what the University knew went to what was reasonable. Whether universities owe students a common-law duty of care was left undecided.

  • EHRC advice note (July 2024)

    The Commission, which intervened in the appeal, advises that methods of assessment ‘will rarely, if ever’ be competence standards. Providers can have constructive knowledge from what a student says and does. Staff must consider adjustments themselves, promptly, with escalation before imminent assessments and resits without an academic appeal if adjustments come too late. Check with professional bodies whether they set the standard or only the method (Equality and Human Rights Commission, 2024).

  • OIA: vivas in AI misconduct cases (July 2025)

    The provider must prove the allegation, share all relevant evidence, including detection reports, and let students explain how they prepared their work. A viva may test understanding, but students should know its focus, time since submission should be considered, and records should be reliable and accurate (OIA, 2025a).

    In one upheld complaint, an international student was penalised after a detector flag and a viva that tested subject knowledge but not how they had worked; the evidence was not shared beforehand, and the decision rested on the viva alone (OIA, 2025b).

  • OfS condition B4 (England)

    Registered providers must ensure students are assessed effectively and each assessment is valid and reliable (Office for Students, 2022). Moderated orals help you show that. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own quality arrangements.

  • What to check, and with whom

    Quality office: whether a viva can confirm, adjust or gate a mark, published before the task.

    Disability service and equality team: the alternative for each oral, and an equality impact assessment.

    Professional body: whether oral performance is a competence standard.

    Data protection officer: recording and retention.

    Misconduct lead: how an assessment viva hands over to an investigation.
