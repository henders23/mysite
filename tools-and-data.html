Choosing AI tools and protecting data in UK HE

What to check before you use an AI tool in teaching or ask students to use one: licences, data, copyright, accessibility and equity, with a checklist you can copy. This is practical guidance, not legal advice, and it tells you whom to ask.

Please note. These are suggestions and directions for exploration, not settled answers. They draw on published research and practice but have not been peer reviewed. Adapt what you use to your context, and check your institution’s policies before acting on it.

Licensed tool or personal account: why the difference matters

The same chatbot can sit under two very different sets of terms. With an institutional licence, your university has a contract with the provider. That contract covers where data is stored, who can see it, how long it is kept and whether it is used to train models. Someone has usually reviewed security and accessibility, and often there is a data protection impact assessment (DPIA). With a personal account, you have accepted consumer terms as an individual, and your institution has no agreement with the provider at all.

That difference decides what you can safely do. On a personal account, conversations may be kept and, depending on the provider and your settings, used to improve future models. If you paste in information about students or colleagues, it has left your institution’s controls, and your data protection team may need to treat it as an incident.

So the first question is not ‘which tool is best?’ but ‘which tools has my institution approved, and for what?’ Approvals usually cover named uses. A licensed tool cleared for drafting teaching materials has not necessarily been cleared for marking or feedback on student work.

What never goes into a chatbot

Keep these out of any AI tool unless your institution has approved that tool for that use, and check the approval really covers it.

  • Student work with identifiersNames, student numbers, email addresses, or details that reveal who wrote it. A student’s draft is both their personal data and their intellectual property. Deleting the name is often not enough: if you can still link the text to the student, it remains personal data in your hands (ICO, no date).
  • Special category dataUK GDPR gives extra protection to data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs or trade union membership, and to genetic data, biometric data used for identification, and data about health, sex life or sexual orientation (ICO, 2024). In teaching it turns up in disability adjustments, extenuating circumstances claims, wellbeing notes, and reflective writing where students disclose health or identity. Criminal offence data has its own rules. Treat all of this as off limits unless your data protection officer has said otherwise.
  • Unpublished research and confidential materialParticipant data collected under consent forms that did not mention AI, unpublished findings, partners’ commercially sensitive data, and grant applications or manuscripts you are reviewing. UKRI, for example, tells assessors not to use generative AI in assessment except to refine their own language, and never to enter any part of the application (UKRI, 2024). Check the journal’s reviewer policy before using AI on a manuscript.
  • Assessment and staff materialUnreleased exam papers and marking schemes, references, and anything from recruitment, HR or misconduct processes.

Approved is not the same as anything goes

A licensed tool lowers the risk but leaves the rules in place. Enter only what the task needs, remove identifiers where you can, and tell students in advance when their work will be processed by AI.

When a DPIA is needed

UK GDPR requires a DPIA before any processing that is likely to result in a high risk to people’s rights and freedoms. The ICO’s view is that ‘in the vast majority of cases’ using AI will involve processing of this kind. Where an organisation decides that a particular use is not high risk, it must still record how it reached that view (ICO, 2023).

You are not expected to write a DPIA yourself. Your job is to spot when a plan might need one and to raise it early with your institution’s data protection officer (DPO). Typical triggers are AI feedback on student work at scale, any AI role in marking or progression decisions, special category data, recording or transcribing students, and any tool that is not on the approved list. Ask too whether an existing DPIA for a licensed tool already covers what you plan.

The law is changing under you. By mid-2026 most of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025’s changes to data protection law were in force, and guidance is being updated in stages (ICO, 2026). On 30 September 2026 the ICO’s functions passed to a new Information Commission (Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport, 2026). For the current position, ask your DPO rather than relying on older summaries, this one included.

Copyright and student work

Students normally own the copyright in what they write. The author of a work is its first owner, and the main exception is work made by an employee in the course of employment (Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, s. 11). Some institutions’ regulations change this for particular kinds of work, so check yours. Uploading a student’s essay to an AI tool copies it, and institutions differ on whether their regulations already cover that. Ask before you assume. Whatever the answer, it is good practice to tell students in advance and to offer a route that does not involve AI.

Licensed readings raise a separate question. Scanned chapters and articles come under licences with their own terms. At the time of writing, the Copyright Licensing Agency’s published permissions for putting licensed content into generative AI prompts cover a list of business and public-sector licences, apply only to paid-for enterprise tools, and do not include the Higher Education Licence (Copyright Licensing Agency, no date). Ask your library before you paste licensed text into any tool.

UK law on AI training is unsettled. In Getty Images v Stability AI, the High Court rejected Getty’s secondary copyright claim, finding that the image model was not an ‘infringing copy’. Getty had already dropped its main training claim, because the training took place outside the UK (Getty Images (US) Inc and others v Stability AI Ltd [2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch)). Getty was then given permission to appeal on the ‘infringing copy’ point (Jewson and Roberts, 2025). In March 2026 the government said that a broad copyright exception with an opt-out was no longer its preferred way forward, and that it would gather more evidence before changing the law (DSIT, DCMS and IPO, 2026).

Ownership of AI output is uncertain too. The 1988 Act protects ‘computer-generated’ works and names as author the person who made the arrangements for creating them (Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, s. 9(3)). How that applies to generative AI is unclear. The government’s March 2026 report proposed removing this protection, while copyright would continue for work made with AI assistance (DSIT, DCMS and IPO, 2026). The practical upshot for teaching is to present the law to students as open, and to follow your institution’s policy before you publish AI-assisted work.

Accessibility

Anything you publish to students, including an AI tool, a course chatbot or an AI-made resource, falls under the same accessibility law as the rest of your teaching materials. The Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications) (No. 2) Accessibility Regulations 2018 cover public sector websites, apps, intranets and extranets. Jisc’s guidance is that colleges and universities are within scope, including virtual learning environments, documents, and third-party content the institution pays for or commissions (Jisc, no date). The standard to meet is WCAG 2.2 AA (Government Digital Service and Central Digital and Data Office, 2024).

In practice, this means three things. Ask suppliers for an accessibility statement before adoption. Have a person review AI-generated captions, transcripts, alt text and slides before you publish them. And remember that a tool you build and publish through your institution is covered as well.

Disability matters in the other direction too. Many disabled students use AI as assistive technology, and the duty to make reasonable adjustments is anticipatory: institutions are expected to plan for disabled students in general rather than wait for individual requests (Equality and Human Rights Commission, 2024). Before you ban AI across a module, check the ban with your disability service.

Asking students to use tools

Requiring a tool is a design decision with equity costs. In HEPI’s 2026 survey of 1,054 UK undergraduates, only 38% said they were provided with AI tools. HEPI recommends that any AI tools needed for course content or assessment be accessible to all students (Stephenson and Armstrong, 2026).

  • Use the licensed tool, or noneIf a task needs AI, build it on a tool your institution provides. If there isn’t one, make AI use optional, or demonstrate the tool yourself and give students the outputs to work on.
  • Design for the free tierPaid tiers offer stronger models, higher limits and features such as file upload. If a task only works well on a paid tier, students who pay are doing an easier task. Test it on the version a student with no money would use.
  • Don’t make students open personal accountsThat would require them to accept a provider’s terms and hand over personal data as a condition of your course. Always offer another way in.
  • Offer a non-AI routeSome students object on ethical, environmental or privacy grounds, and some cannot use a particular tool for accessibility reasons. Give them outputs to critique or an equivalent task without the tool, and assess both routes against the same learning outcomes.
  • Say what may go inTell students what they may paste and what they may not: no other students’ work, no personal details, and nothing from placements, clinical settings or research participants.

Before you adopt a tool: ten questions

Use this before you build a tool into a module or ask students to use one. If any answer is ‘don’t know’, send the form to your learning technology team or DPO before you go ahead.

Tool: [name, and the version or tier students will use]
Intended use: [what you and your students will do with it]

1. Licence – Is it institutionally licensed or approved for this specific use? [yes / no / don’t know]
2. Data in – What will be entered? Any personal data, student work or special category data? [list]
3. Training – Are inputs used to train or improve models, and can this be switched off for every user? [answer]
4. Retention – How long are inputs and outputs kept, where, and who can see them? [answer]
5. DPIA – Does an existing DPIA cover this use, or does the DPO need to see it? [answer]
6. Accounts – Do students need personal accounts, or must they give personal details? [answer]
7. Cost – Does the task work on the free or licensed version that every student can get? [answer]
8. Accessibility – Is there an accessibility statement? Does the tool work with a screen reader and a keyboard alone? [answer]
9. Alternative – What will a student who cannot or will not use the tool do instead? [answer]
10. Purpose – What learning does the tool support, and what thinking must students still do themselves? [answer]

Decision: [adopt / pilot / reject]
Reviewed by: [name, role, date]

Where Jisc fits

Jisc supports UK colleges and universities with digital services, licensing and guidance, and three of its AI resources are worth knowing. Its artificial intelligence pages gather guidance on teaching, assessment, policy and strategy, along with sector pilots such as a year-long project, begun in September 2025, testing AI marking and feedback tools. Its AI maturity toolkit helps an institution see where it stands, from ‘approaching and understanding’ to ‘optimised/transformed’, and covers staff support, academic integrity and safe and responsible use. Jisc also negotiates licence agreements on behalf of UK higher education, which is often how institutions get approved tools on sector terms.

Use these to inform your institution’s decisions. They do not replace your own institution’s approved-tools list or your DPO’s advice.
