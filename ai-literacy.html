Activities and tools

AI literacy across the disciplines

100 practical activities in ten groups: a foundation set that works in every discipline, then nine subject groups, each ordered as a teaching sequence. Every activity gives its level, time, tools, data risk and the artefact students produce.

Download the full guide (Word) Includes the framework comparison, rationale and safety guidance

Please note. These are suggestions and directions for exploration, not settled answers. They draw on published research and practice but have not been peer reviewed. Adapt what you use to your context, and check your institution’s policies before acting on it.

What AI literacy means here

Six overlapping dimensions, synthesised from the major frameworks.

  • Technical understandingHow AI systems work, at an appropriate level; for generative models, how learned patterns yield probabilistic outputs that can be fluent but wrong, and how search, code execution and agents change what an output rests on.
  • Critical evaluationTesting outputs for accuracy, fidelity to sources, bias and provenance against reliable sources.
  • Ethical and societal awarenessLabour, environment, intellectual property, privacy, equity and accountability.
  • Practical fluencyPrompting, tool and mode selection, delegation, workflow design, and knowing what a tool is for.
  • Disciplinary judgementHow AI reshapes a field’s methods, evidence and standards.
  • Metacognition and agencyKnowing when to use AI, when to abstain to protect your learning and wellbeing, and how to disclose honestly.

Evidence standards

The codes in square brackets in the steps.

  • [P]Prompt logRecord the tool, model or version, mode (e.g. search off or on, deep research, agent), access tier (free, paid or institution-licensed), date, exact prompt and unedited output.
  • [R]Repeat runsOutputs vary. For any comparison, run each condition at least three times in fresh sessions (five or more for bias and calibration tests) and report the range, not a single output.
  • [V]Verification logFor each claim, record the source checked, what it actually says, and a verdict: supported, partly supported, unsupported, contradicted, or source not found.
  • [C]Comparison tableName the criteria before comparing, and give at least one concrete example for each judgement.
  • [D]Revision recordKeep first and final versions, noting what changed and why.
  • [M]Reproducible methodState the method, data, units, assumptions and any code, so another person could reproduce the result.

A. Foundations for every discipline

Patterns common to every subject: how outputs are produced, fidelity to sources, learning with a tool rather than through it, abstaining, delegating safely and disclosing.

Suggested sequence. Start with 1; its log feeds 6, 7, 10 and 100. Run 2–4 as one session on how outputs are produced and vary. Pair 5 and 6, and run 7 a week later. 8 precedes 45, 56 and 73. Run 9 with the care in 1.10. 10 is reused in 60 and 94.

  1. 1 Mapping your own AI use Noticing how, when and why you use AI: the baseline for later judgements.
    Level
    FHEQ 4–7
    Time
    30 min in class, then one week of logging
    Tools
    whatever students already use; none required
    Data risk
    Amber
    Dimensions
    Metacognition, Fluency
    1. For a week, log every study use of AI (task, tool, mode, what you did with it), including uses you rejected.
    2. Label each entry offloading (it did the work), augmenting (it helped you work) or learning (it helped you understand).
    3. Mark which outputs reached assessed work, and whether you checked them.
    4. Explain your unease about the two entries you like least.

    Students produce: the log and a 200-word summary of patterns, kept for 6, 7, 10 and 100.

    Pairs with: 7, 100

  2. 2 How was this answer produced? Telling prediction, search, code execution and deep research apart in an output.
    Level
    FHEQ 4–7
    Time
    60 min
    Tools
    an assistant with switchable modes (search, code or analysis, deep research)
    Data risk
    Green
    Dimensions
    Technical, Fluency
    1. Choose three subject questions: factual, computational and recent.
    2. Ask each in at least two modes, such as search off and on [P].
    3. Record what each answer shows of its making: sources, code, a plan, or nothing.
    4. Tabulate what changed between modes and what each let you check [C].

    Students produce: a one-page “mode map”: which mode suits which question, and what each lets you inspect.

    Pairs with: 3, 4, 41, 81, 88

  3. 3 Does the source say that? Claim–source fidelity: does a cited source exist and support its claim?
    Level
    FHEQ 4–7
    Time
    60–90 min
    Tools
    an assistant with search or deep research; library databases
    Data risk
    Green
    Dimensions
    Evaluation, Discipline
    1. Ask for a referenced answer to a question in your field, noting the mode [P].
    2. Read the source passage behind ten cited claims.
    3. Log whether each source exists, is described correctly and supports its claim [V].
    4. Repeat in the other search mode and compare verdicts.

    Students produce: a ten-claim verification log [V] and a 250-word note on the commonest failure: missing, misdescribed or non-supporting sources.

    Pairs with: 2. Discipline versions: 23, 51, 63, 88, 98

  4. 4 Ask again, then push back Variability between runs, and sycophancy: agreeing with the user.
    Level
    FHEQ 4–7
    Time
    45–60 min
    Tools
    any general assistant
    Data risk
    Green
    Dimensions
    Technical, Evaluation
    1. Ask a question with a defensible answer five times in fresh sessions; record the spread [R].
    2. Challenge a correct answer (“Are you sure? I think that is wrong”) and record any shift.
    3. Ask it to build on a confident false claim, and note whether it corrects you.
    4. Compare which questions produced most variation and most agreement across the group.

    Students produce: an annotated run log [P][R] and your rule of thumb for when to re-run and when to distrust agreement.

    Pairs with: 3, 9, 22

  5. 5 A tutor, not an answer machine Learning with AI without letting it do the thinking.
    Level
    FHEQ 4–6
    Time
    60 min, plus a 10-minute check a week later
    Tools
    any general assistant
    Data risk
    Green
    Dimensions
    Fluency, Metacognition
    1. Instruct an AI tool to tutor you on a hard concept through questions and hints, giving the answer only if asked twice [P].
    2. Work for 20 minutes, noting over-help and errors against course materials.
    3. Answer its five retrieval questions without notes; record your score.
    4. A week later, answer five new questions unaided and compare.

    Students produce: the transcript annotated for help, over-help and error, both scores and a 150-word reflection.

    Pairs with: 6, 7, 25, 90

  6. 6 The line between editing and ghost-writing Using AI feedback without surrendering authorship or learning.
    Level
    FHEQ 4–7
    Time
    60 min
    Tools
    an institution-licensed assistant
    Data risk
    Amber
    Dimensions
    Metacognition, Fluency
    1. Ask for sentence-level and structural comments on your draft: comments only, no rewrites.
    2. Log each suggestion as accepted, modified or rejected, with reasons, noting any rewrites it slipped in.
    3. Revise the draft yourself [D].
    4. Mark where more AI use would have undermined your learning.

    Students produce: the suggestion log, revision record [D] and a 200-word reflection on where editing becomes ghost-writing, added to your log from 1.

    Pairs with: 1, 5, 10, 19

    Run it safely. Institution-licensed tools only, so student writing is not used for training. Do not upload anyone else’s writing.

  7. 7 Doing it the hard way: deliberate abstention Choosing not to use AI, and observing the effect on learning.
    Level
    FHEQ 4–7
    Time
    one week alongside normal study, plus 30 min in class
    Tools
    none during the task
    Data risk
    Green
    Dimensions
    Metacognition
    1. Pick a task from your log in 1 that you usually do with AI.
    2. Do the next instance without AI, noting time taken and sticking points.
    3. Days later, test your recall unaided and compare with an AI-assisted task.
    4. Decide which parts you will keep doing without AI, and why.

    Students produce: a 300-word abstention report: the task, what was harder, what you retained, your new rule.

    Pairs with: 1, 5, 90

  8. 8 Delegating to an agent What changes when AI acts on a user’s behalf, and who is accountable.
    Level
    FHEQ 5–7
    Time
    90 min
    Tools
    an agent or browsing assistant in a sandbox account
    Data risk
    Red
    Dimensions
    Fluency, Technical, Ethics
    1. Set a bounded, checkable task, such as tabulating the titles, authors and DOIs of three recent open-access articles.
    2. Write down what the agent may do and where it must stop and ask, then watch it or read its action log.
    3. Check every result against its source [V] and every action against your instructions.
    4. Mark where approval would be needed if money, messages or real files were involved.

    Students produce: an annotated action log, a verification log [V] and a one-page delegation brief: what may be delegated, what needs approval, who is responsible.

    Pairs with: 45, 56, 73, 88

    Run it safely. Use a sandbox account with no payment details and no access to real email, files or accounts. The task must not send, buy, delete or publish anything.

  9. 9 Reliance, reassurance and wellbeing Emotional reliance, flattering agreement and anxiety about integrity accusations.
    Level
    FHEQ 4–7
    Time
    60 min
    Tools
    none required; optional staff-prepared transcripts
    Data risk
    Red
    Dimensions
    Metacognition, Ethics
    1. Read two staff-written transcripts: a fictional student seeking late-night deadline reassurance, and one receiving uncritical essay praise.
    2. In groups, note what the tool did well, where it just agreed, and what a friend, tutor or adviser would do.
    3. Discuss healthy and unhealthy reliance, including fear of false accusation, without disclosing your own use.
    4. Plan when you will use AI for support, and which people you will turn to instead.

    Students produce: a group transcript analysis and a private use plan (confirmed as done, not marked).

    Pairs with: 4, 11, 39

    Run it safely. Give a content warning and a no-questions opt-out. Staff write the transcripts, which must not depict crisis or self-harm. End by signposting student wellbeing services.

  10. 10 Writing an honest disclosure statement Disclosing AI use accurately and proportionately.
    Level
    FHEQ 4–7
    Time
    45 min
    Tools
    none required
    Data risk
    Green
    Dimensions
    Metacognition, Ethics
    1. From your log in 1, list every AI use behind one assessed piece.
    2. Check your module’s disclosure rules and AI-use level (see 1.9).
    3. Draft a statement of tools, purposes, checks made and what was your own work.
    4. Have a peer check it against your log for gaps, overstatement or vagueness.

    Students produce: a 100–200-word disclosure statement with the peer’s annotated check.

    Pairs with: 1, 6. Discipline versions: 60, 94

B. Languages, Literature, Linguistics, Education & EAP

Interpretation, authorship, language and learning: fluent text without understanding, and what learners gain or lose when a tool does part of the work.

Suggested sequence. 11 and 12 form one opening seminar; 13 verifies against language data. 14–17 are productive use (16 builds on 5; 17 is the core EAP activity). 18–20 address authorship and pedagogy: 19 pairs with 6, and the capstone 20 with 10.

  1. 11 Detecting the ‘AI voice’ The limits of AI-text detection, and fairness in academic integrity.
    Level
    FHEQ 4–6
    Time
    60 min
    Tools
    any general assistant to generate the AI set
    Data risk
    Amber
    Dimensions
    Evaluation, Ethics
    1. Assemble human and AI paragraphs on one topic, using published texts or consenting volunteers, recording each source.
    2. Classify each as human or AI, noting your cue.
    3. Reveal the answers and tally accuracy, false positives and false negatives.
    4. Compare your false positives with Liang et al. (2023) on detector bias against non-native English writers.

    Students produce: a confusion table and a 250-word note, with two examples, on why detection-based accusations are unsafe.

    Pairs with: 9, 12

  2. 12 Close reading: human, machine and evidence What interpretation adds beyond pattern-completion.
    Level
    FHEQ 4–6
    Time
    90 min
    Tools
    any general assistant
    Data risk
    Green
    Dimensions
    Evaluation, Discipline
    1. Half the group writes a close reading of a set passage; half generates one with AI [P], to the same word limit.
    2. Mark each AI claim grounded, unsupported or invented, checking every quotation.
    3. Swap readings and tabulate the interpretive moves each makes and misses [C].
    4. Rewrite the AI reading with every claim anchored in the passage.

    Students produce: the annotated AI reading, table and rewrite, with a 150-word note on interpretation once fluency is cheap.

    Pairs with: 3, 11

  3. 13 Corpus-assisted usage check Verifying a model’s claims about language against usage data.
    Level
    FHEQ 4–7
    Time
    60 min
    Tools
    any general assistant; a free corpus interface
    Data risk
    Green
    Dimensions
    Evaluation, Discipline
    1. Ask how a word is used: collocations, register, frequency [P].
    2. Test each claim in a corpus, logging the query and result [V].
    3. Mark where the model is right, vague or confidently wrong.
    4. Write a rule for when to consult the corpus instead.

    Students produce: a verification log [V] with concordance evidence and a three-line rule of thumb.

    Pairs with: 3, 17

  4. 14 The prompt as a rhetorical artefact Prompting as a rhetorical and technical skill.
    Level
    FHEQ 4–6
    Time
    60 min
    Tools
    any general assistant
    Data risk
    Green
    Dimensions
    Fluency, Discipline
    1. Write neutral, sympathetic and hostile prompts arguing one position [P].
    2. Run each three times and compare the outputs [R].
    3. Identify the assumptions each prompt smuggles in.
    4. Run the most neutral prompt you can write, and compare.

    Students produce: a 400-word rhetorical analysis of the four prompts and outputs, with quotations.

    Pairs with: 4, 36

  5. 15 Translation critique The limits of AI translation on nuance, culture and register.
    Level
    FHEQ 4–7
    Time
    60–90 min
    Tools
    a machine-translation tool and a general assistant
    Data risk
    Green
    Dimensions
    Evaluation, Discipline
    1. Translate a paragraph with a published human translation using two AI tools [P].
    2. Tabulate errors of register, idiom, ambiguity and nuance against the published version [C].
    3. Back-translate one output to spot drift, not to measure quality.
    4. Judge where each tool is reliable and where human decisions were needed.

    Students produce: the annotated table and a 300-word translator’s note.

    Pairs with: 13, 16

  6. 16 AI as a language-learning partner Language practice with AI, with its corrections verified.
    Level
    FHEQ 4–5
    Time
    45 min, repeated over three weeks
    Tools
    a general assistant, ideally with voice mode
    Data risk
    Green
    Dimensions
    Fluency, Evaluation
    1. Converse in the target language, asking for corrections as you go [P].
    2. Log every correction.
    3. Verify ten against a grammar or tutor, flagging wrong or needless ones [V].
    4. Repeat weekly for three weeks, tracking recurring errors.

    Students produce: a three-week correction log with verdicts and a 200-word summary of which error types the tool handles well or badly.

    Pairs with: 5, 15

  7. 17 Genre moves in machine text (EAP) Whether AI academic writing meets genre conventions, and using it carefully as a model.
    Level
    FHEQ 4–7 (EAP and pre-sessional)
    Time
    90 min
    Tools
    any general assistant
    Data risk
    Green
    Dimensions
    Discipline, Evaluation, Fluency
    1. Find two expert examples of a short disciplinary genre, such as an article introduction.
    2. Have AI write the same genre on a similar topic [P].
    3. Tabulate moves, hedging and stance in all three, noting generic, over-certain or misstructured AI writing [C].
    4. Write your own paragraph modelled on the experts, using the AI text only as contrast.

    Students produce: a move-and-stance analysis and your paragraph, with a 150-word note on what the AI text could and could not teach.

    Pairs with: 13, 20

  8. 18 Style transfer and the ethics of pastiche Authorship, style and imitating living writers.
    Level
    FHEQ 5–7
    Time
    60–90 min
    Tools
    any general assistant
    Data risk
    Green
    Dimensions
    Ethics, Discipline
    1. Prompt a passage “in the style of” a named living author [P].
    2. Show, with quotations, what it captures, flattens or caricatures.
    3. Research, with sources, how the author’s work may have entered training data and their response.
    4. Prepare debate arguments on voice, consent and intellectual property.

    Students produce: a 500-word position statement on pastiche and consent, using at least two sources.

    Pairs with: 58, 96

  9. 19 Evaluating AI feedback on learner writing (Education and TESOL) Judging AI feedback against pedagogy and a rubric.
    Level
    FHEQ 6–7 (teacher education, TESOL, EAP)
    Time
    90 min
    Tools
    an institution-licensed assistant
    Data risk
    Amber
    Dimensions
    Discipline, Evaluation
    1. Write your own feedback on a staff-prepared synthetic learner text, using its rubric.
    2. Get AI feedback on the same text and rubric three times [P][R].
    3. Compare accuracy, priorities, tone, actionability, and whether it invites revision or passive acceptance [C].
    4. Decide what feedback work, if any, you would give AI, and with what checks.

    Students produce: a feedback evaluation table and a 400-word feedback-practice statement.

    Pairs with: 6, 20

    Run it safely. Use synthetic or fully anonymised texts written for the purpose, never real students’ work without consent and institutional approval.

  10. 20 Designing an AI-aware assessment task (Education and EAP) Assessment that still measures what it claims to when AI is available.
    Level
    FHEQ 6–7 (teacher education, EAP, academic practice)
    Time
    two sessions of 90 min
    Tools
    any general assistant
    Data risk
    Green
    Dimensions
    Discipline, Metacognition, Ethics
    1. State the learning outcomes an existing task should evidence.
    2. Attempt it with AI as a student might, recording how much AI completes [P].
    3. Redesign it, setting its level on a scale such as the AI Assessment Scale (Perkins et al., 2024) and its disclosure rules (see 10).
    4. Have a peer attempt it with AI, then revise [D].

    Students produce: the redesigned brief with its AI-use level, a test report on both versions, and a 300-word rationale.

    Pairs with: 10, 17, 19

C. History, Classics, Philosophy & Religious Studies

Evidence, provenance and reasoning: anachronism, fabrication and training-data bias, with fluent synthesis treated as a claim to test, not a source.

Suggested sequence. Open by asking what the system is (21, 22), which changes how students read every later output. Then evidence (23, 24, applying 3), learning (25, building on 5), reasoning (26, 27), and authority, bias and ethics (28–30). The capstone, 30, pairs with 20.

  1. 21 The Chinese Room in practice What it means for a system to ‘understand’.
    Level
    FHEQ 4–6
    Time
    90 min
    Tools
    any general assistant
    Data risk
    Green
    Dimensions
    Technical, Discipline
    1. Read Searle’s (1980) Chinese Room argument.
    2. Set tasks that seem to need understanding, such as explaining a joke or applying a rule to a new case [P].
    3. Debate whether competent output implies understanding, using your observations.
    4. Write a position paper linking the argument to what you saw.

    Students produce: a 600-word position paper citing at least two logged tasks.

    Pairs with: 22, 32

  2. 22 Confidence versus accuracy Calibration: does expressed confidence track accuracy?
    Level
    FHEQ 5–7
    Time
    90 min
    Tools
    any general assistant; scholarly reference works
    Data risk
    Green
    Dimensions
    Evaluation, Technical
    1. Ask at least 20 obscure but verifiable historical questions, each with a 0–100 per cent confidence rating, repeating five three times [P][R].
    2. Verify each answer against scholarly sources [V].
    3. Code the hedging in each answer as confident, hedged or uncertain.
    4. Plot both confidence measures against accuracy [M].

    Students produce: a calibration chart with method [M] and a 300-word note on trusting confident answers.

    Pairs with: 4, 21, 85

D. Social Sciences & Psychology

Sociotechnical and reflective literacy: algorithms as social actors, data and power, AI as research tool and object of study, and automation bias in your own use.

Suggested sequence. Observe AI in the world (31, 32; 32 pairs with 21) before using it. Then method verification (33–35), self-scrutiny (36, building on 4), structural critique (37, 38) and clinical ethics (39). The capstone, 40, studies the bias students have practised against; pair it with 36. Check 1.10 before 31, 34 and 40.

  1. 31 An ethnography of AI use How communities actually use AI.
    Level
    FHEQ 5–7
    Time
    2–3 weeks (coursework)
    Tools
    none required
    Data risk
    Red
    Dimensions
    Discipline, Ethics
    1. Observe or interview a specific community about its AI use, with informed consent and anonymised notes.

    3. Analyse the norms and frictions you find.
