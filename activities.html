Assessment redesigns

Discipline by discipline: what the assessment really certifies, what AI disrupts, redesigned tasks, and where to secure the evidence.

Discipline group

26 disciplines

Humanities and social sciences

  • English literature and literary studies Certifies: Constructing a defensible interpretation of a text through close attention to its language, form and context, and sustaining it against alternatives.

    What AI disrupts. The interpretive essay on a canonical text is the most heavily represented genre in training data, so models produce competent, conventional readings instantly. They do less well at sustained attention to a detail that resists the standard reading, and at accountability to a specific critical conversation.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. The resistant detailStudents choose a passage of no more than 150 words that complicates the dominant critical reading and build a 1,500-word argument from it, engaging two named critics, plus a 200-word note on how the passage was found and what was rejected. The value lies in the selection and the friction between detail and consensus, and the note makes the search assessable.
    2. The critical edition apparatusStudents produce an annotated teaching edition of a short text, with headnote, glosses, discussion questions and a rationale for every editorial choice. Editorial judgement is selective and consequential, and the rationale for omission is where the thinking shows.
    3. Adversarial readingStudents write a rebuttal of an AI-generated interpretation of a set text, showing where it is textually unsupported, where it substitutes plausibility for evidence and what it fails to notice. This directly assesses evaluative judgement against disciplinary standards.
    Where to secure the evidence A 15-minute interpretive oral on an unseen short passage from a studied author, after 20 minutes’ preparation, with follow-up questions that probe close reading and thinking on one’s feet.

    Draws on Bearman et al. (2024).

  • History Certifies: Constructing causal and interpretive arguments from incomplete, biased primary evidence, with explicit reasoning about the status of sources.

    What AI disrupts. Synthesis of secondary literature, the bulk of the traditional undergraduate essay. Models also fabricate archival references convincingly, a serious hazard for the discipline.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. The archive boxStudents receive a small set of digitised primary documents and build an argument on a specific question, explicitly discussing what the sources cannot tell us. Locality and evidential constraint do the work, and epistemic honesty carries the weight.
    2. The historiographical auditStudents audit an AI-generated account of an event: which interpretive school it reflects, what it silences, which claims are unsupported and what a historian of another tradition would say. Making the model’s dominant historiography visible is both critical AI literacy and historiographical training.
    3. Citation verification exerciseStudents verify each of twelve references in a short essay, several fabricated or misattributed, and report on them. Verification is now a core, assessable research skill.
    Where to secure the evidence A document-based examination or supervised source analysis on an unseen primary source: traditional in form, but now doing precise work rather than blanket security.
  • Philosophy Certifies: Constructing valid arguments, identifying hidden premises, and following an argument where it leads under pressure.

    What AI disrupts. Exposition of existing positions and standard objections, the scaffolding of most undergraduate essays — though models are notably weak at sustaining a novel position under sequential objection.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. The dialectical exchangeA three-round written exchange: state a position, receive an objection, respond in 500 words, receive a second-order objection, respond again. Assessed on the trajectory, so the mark rewards responsiveness rather than polish.
    2. Steelmanning the position you rejectStudents write the strongest version of an argument they disagree with, then pinpoint the exact premise where they part company. This assesses charity and precision, and the located disagreement is hard to delegate.
    3. Argument reconstruction from a bad sourceStudents formalise a fluent but invalid AI-generated argument and identify where validity fails. Fluent invalidity is exactly what models produce and what philosophy trains students to detect.
    Where to secure the evidence An oral defence or supervised seminar performance assessed against criteria, making the tutorial explicit and criterion-referenced.
  • Modern languages and translation studies Certifies: Communicative competence in the target language; for translation, defensible decisions under competing constraints of register, culture and function.

    What AI disrupts. Machine translation now clears professional thresholds for many text types, and written composition in the target language is largely unsecurable outside supervised conditions.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. The translation commentaryThe translation carries limited marks; most go to a commentary justifying each significant decision against purpose, register, audience and culture. The profession has moved to post-editing, so the construct becomes judgement rather than production.
    2. Post-editing under specificationStudents post-edit a machine translation against a demanding client brief and document every change with a rationale. This is contemporary professional practice.
    3. Culturally embedded interpretationStudents analyse a text whose meaning depends on cultural knowledge models handle poorly — humour, dialect, political allusion — and explain what is lost in machine rendering.
    Where to secure the evidence An oral proficiency interview and live interpreting or supervised written production. Spoken competence is the discipline’s central claim and can’t be secured any other way.
  • EAP and academic literacies (in-sessional) Certifies: Participating in the meaning-making practices of a target discipline, including but not reducible to control of its genres, register and citation.

    What AI disrupts. Almost every product-focused EAP assessment. More deeply, it forces the field to confront whether it was assessing language as a proxy for participation — vindicating the academic literacies position by making the proxy worthless.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Disciplinary genre analysisStudents collect three texts from their own discipline, analyse move structure, hedging, citation function and stance, and explain what counts as a persuasive claim there. It assesses noticing and analysis, grounded in the student’s own field.
    2. The revision dossierStudents submit a draft, an AI critique of it, their revision, and a commentary on which suggestions they accepted or rejected and why. The highest-value EAP task in an AI era: the reasoned rejections are the most diagnostic evidence available.
    3. Source-use forensicsStudents judge whether each source in a paragraph is used accurately, whether its citation function matches the claim, and whether any claim is unsupported. Citation misuse is the characteristic AI failure and a long-standing EAP teaching point.
    4. Live text reconstructionIn class, students read and discuss a source, put it away, write from notes, then compare with the original. Supervised and low-stakes, it assesses reading-to-write directly.
    Where to secure the evidence Supervised writing from unseen sources plus a short tutorial on the student’s own text — which, done well, protects multilingual students rather than policing them.

    Draws on Lea and Street (1998); Tai et al. (2018); Carless and Boud (2018).

  • Law Certifies: Identifying operative legal issues in unstructured facts, applying authority accurately, and advising under uncertainty.

    What AI disrupts. The problem question and the doctrinal essay, the two staple genres. Hallucinated citations have produced real judicial sanctions, making verification a professional competence.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. The unstructured client fileInstead of a tidy problem question, a messy file — a rambling email, contradictory accounts, an incomplete contract, an irrelevant invoice. Students identify what matters and what to ask next, then advise.
    2. Authority verification and risk memoStudents verify and correct an AI-drafted advice note containing plausible but wrong authority, and write a memo on the professional-conduct risk of relying on it.
    3. Advice under changing instructionsStudents advise, then the client’s instructions change materially and they must revise and explain the consequences. It assesses adaptive reasoning, not a single output.
    Where to secure the evidence An interactive oral advising a simulated client or supervising partner on an unseen scenario, with unpredictable follow-up questions.

    Draws on Head and Willis (2024).

  • Business, management and marketing Certifies: Diagnosing organisational problems in context and justifying decisions under constraint, uncertainty and competing interests.

    What AI disrupts. Almost the entire traditional diet — SWOT analyses, marketing plans, case reports — which models perform convincingly because they are formulaic and reward coverage.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. The live clientStudents work with a real local organisation, gather primary data and deliver recommendations; the client’s questions form part of the assessment. The data doesn’t exist online.
    2. The constrained decisionWith a hard budget, a fixed deadline and a stakeholder harmed by any option, students must choose, justify, and say what they’d monitor to know if they were wrong. It rewards commitment over balanced coverage.
    3. Assumption audit of an AI strategyStudents audit an AI-generated strategy for a real firm against its financial statements and market data, documenting where it is generic, unfounded or dated.
    Where to secure the evidence A panel-based interactive oral defence of the recommendation, with questions from staff and, where possible, the client.
  • Economics Certifies: Modelling economic phenomena, interpreting empirical results, and reasoning about identification, mechanism and policy consequence.

    What AI disrupts. Standard derivations, textbook problem sets, policy essays, and increasingly the mechanical parts of applied econometrics.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. The identification critiqueStudents critique the identification strategy of a published paper or AI-generated study design: the counterfactual, what threatens it, and what test would distinguish the hypotheses.
    2. Local data, contested questionStudents use a real dataset to answer a policy question, reporting every specification tried, not just the one reported. The specification log is the assessable thinking.
    3. Model failure diagnosisStudents find where a model’s answer conflates correlation with causation, ignores general equilibrium effects or stretches a partial model too far.
    Where to secure the evidence Supervised problem-solving for core technical competence, plus an oral defence of the empirical project.
  • Politics, international relations and sociology Certifies: Theoretically informed analysis of social and political phenomena, with reflexive awareness of the analyst’s position.

    What AI disrupts. Theory-application essays and literature-based analysis. Models also carry ideological and geographic defaults — a hazard and a teaching opportunity.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. The model’s politicsStudents prompt a model on a contested question from several framings, analyse the pattern of its answers and situate its assumptions within the discipline’s traditions.
    2. Primary data mini-studyA small qualitative study — three interviews, an observation or document analysis — with a full reflexive account of positionality and limitations.
    3. Contemporaneous analysisStudents analyse an event happening during the module, using sources from the last month, reasoning without an established secondary literature.
    Where to secure the evidence An assessed seminar performance or oral examination probing the student’s own theoretical commitments.

    Draws on Roe, Furze and Perkins (2026).

  • Psychology Certifies: Designing sound studies, interpreting evidence appropriately, and reasoning about measurement, inference and ethics.

    What AI disrupts. Lab reports (formulaic, so highly generable), literature reviews and standard statistical write-ups.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Design-first assessmentStudents produce a pre-registration — hypotheses, design, power analysis, analysis plan, and what would falsify the hypothesis — assessed before any data exists.
    2. Analyse your own cohort dataStudents analyse a class-generated dataset with all its messiness, making real decisions about exclusions and assumptions.
    3. The flawed manuscript reviewStudents review a manuscript seeded with problems — underpowered design, the wrong test, p-hacking, overclaiming — and write a reviewer report.
    Where to secure the evidence A supervised statistical reasoning assessment on interpretation rather than computation, plus an oral defence of the final-year project.
  • Education and teacher training Certifies: Pedagogical reasoning: designing, enacting and adapting teaching in response to actual learners.

    What AI disrupts. Lesson plans, reflective essays and theory-application assignments. Reflective writing is especially vulnerable: formulaic, and unverifiable from text alone.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Plan, teach, analyseStudents teach a lesson, then analyse a recorded segment against specific learner responses, identifying the moment they adapted and why.
    2. AI-planned, professionally repairedStudents critique an AI-generated lesson plan against their actual class: prior knowledge, EAL needs, SEND provision, behaviour and sequence.
    3. Assessment design taskStudents design an assessment for their own subject and phase, with a rationale and a validity argument.
    Where to secure the evidence Observed teaching followed by a professional conversation: already standard in teacher education, and the model other disciplines are converging on.
  • Journalism and media studies Certifies: Verification, source relationships, news judgement and ethical decision-making under deadline.

    What AI disrupts. Copy production, feature writing and press-release reporting, while the industry has made verification and AI-detection literacy central.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Original reporting onlyAssessment requires named human sources the student obtained, backed by a contact log. Source acquisition can’t be generated.
    2. Verification exerciseGiven claims, images and clips of mixed provenance, including synthetic media, students verify them, publish only what is supportable, and document their method.
    3. Ethics under deadlineIn a timed, supervised scenario with an unverified claim, competitive pressure and a vulnerable subject, students decide what to publish and justify it.
    Where to secure the evidence A timed newsroom simulation with editorial questioning, plus a portfolio viva on original reporting.

Science, engineering, computing and mathematics

  • Engineering Certifies: Analysing physical systems, making design decisions under competing constraints, and taking responsibility for outcomes with safety consequences.

    What AI disrupts. Standard analysis problems, design reports and routine calculation. A graduate who can’t spot a physically implausible result is a hazard.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Design with an infeasible constraintThe brief contains a real conflict; students must recognise it can’t be met as stated, propose a resolution and justify the trade-off. Models produce a solution rather than notice the problem is ill-posed.
    2. Sanity-check the machineStudents find embedded errors in generated calculations — a unit error, a false linearity, a missing safety factor — using order-of-magnitude reasoning and first principles.
    3. Build, test, explain the discrepancyStudents predict a prototype’s performance, measure it and account for the gap. The discrepancy is real and unique to their build.
    4. Failure analysis of a real caseStudents reconstruct the decision chain behind a documented engineering failure and identify where judgement was compromised.
    Where to secure the evidence Supervised technical assessment in early years plus a final-year design-project viva on assumptions, load paths, safety factors and materials.
  • Computer science and software engineering Certifies: Reasoning about correctness, complexity, architecture and trade-offs, and producing reliable systems with code-generating tools.

    What AI disrupts. Essentially all take-home programming. Banning AI-assisted coding produces unemployable graduates; ignoring it produces graduates who can’t debug what they ship.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Specify, generate, verifyStudents write a precise specification, generate an implementation with AI, then write tests and an argument for why they’re sufficient. Marks weight the specification and test reasoning.
    2. Debug the plausibleAI-generated code passes naive tests but hides a subtle defect; students find, characterise and fix it, then write a regression test.
    3. Complexity and architecture rationaleStudents justify an architectural choice against alternatives, reasoning about scale, cost and failure modes.
    4. Commit history as artefactProject work is assessed partly on its development history. Histories can be faked, hence the assurance point.
    Where to secure the evidence A 20-minute live code review: the student walks through their own code, explains decisions, and modifies it live for a new requirement.
  • Mathematics Certifies: Constructing and communicating rigorous proofs, and choosing the right abstraction for a real problem.

    What AI disrupts. Routine computation is fully solved. Proof is mixed: models produce convincing but subtly invalid arguments, which is a teaching opportunity.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Proof verification and repairStudents adjudicate several purported proofs, some valid and some subtly flawed, and repair what can be repaired.
    2. Modelling from a messFaced with an unstructured real situation and no stated variables, students decide what to model, justify the abstraction, solve it and critique its limits.
    3. Exposition for a specified audienceStudents explain a non-trivial result to a stated audience and are assessed on the fidelity of the simplification.
    Where to secure the evidence Supervised examination, since the construct really is unaided reasoning, plus a short oral explaining a proof from their own coursework.
  • Physics, chemistry and the laboratory sciences Certifies: Experimental design, measurement judgement, uncertainty reasoning, and connecting theory to observation.

    What AI disrupts. The lab report, which is generable from a protocol and a plausible dataset, and standard theoretical problems.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Your data, your anomalyStudents submit their own raw data and account for its specific anomalies, with that discussion weighted heavily. The anomaly is unique to that bench on that day.
    2. Uncertainty budgetA full uncertainty analysis for their own measurement, identifying which term dominates and what single change would most improve precision.
    3. Design the experimentGiven a question and limited equipment, students design a protocol and predict what would falsify their hypothesis before running anything.
    4. Synthesis route justification (chemistry)Students propose and justify a route on yield, cost, hazard and green-chemistry criteria, then critique an AI-proposed route on the same grounds.
    Where to secure the evidence A practical examination with in-lab questioning against criteria, plus supervised interpretation of unseen data.
  • Biological and biomedical sciences Certifies: Reasoning from experimental evidence to mechanistic claims, with appropriate scepticism about inference.

    What AI disrupts. Literature reviews, essay-based assessment and standard data-interpretation questions.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Figure forensicsGiven figures from a real paper without its text, students state what can and can’t be concluded and identify overclaiming.
    2. The mechanism proposalGiven an unexplained observation, students propose two competing mechanisms and design the experiment that would distinguish them.
    3. Research proposal with peer reviewStudents write a proposal, review a peer’s against funder criteria, then revise their own.
    Where to secure the evidence Supervised unseen data interpretation plus a project viva.

Health, veterinary and professional practice

  • Medicine Certifies: Clinical reasoning: generating and refining differentials from ambiguous presentations, under uncertainty.

    What AI disrupts. Written knowledge assessment and case-based coursework — though medicine is best prepared, having developed programmatic and workplace-based assessment.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Reasoning-visible case analysisStudents document their differential at each stage of an unfolding case, stating what each new piece of information does to their probabilities. Assessed on the trajectory, not the final diagnosis.
    2. Interrogate the decision-support toolGiven an AI diagnostic suggestion, students state what they’d confirm before acting, what would make them override it, and how they’d explain it to the patient.
    3. The uncertainty conversationIn a simulated consultation, students communicate diagnostic uncertainty and negotiate a plan.
    Where to secure the evidence OSCEs, mini-CEX, workplace-based assessments and entrustable professional activities, aggregated towards progression decisions.

    Draws on Pearce and Chiavaroli (2023); van der Vleuten et al. (2012); Bearman and Ajjawi (2023).

  • Nursing, midwifery and allied health Certifies: Safe, person-centred practice: assessment, prioritisation, escalation and communication under real conditions.

    What AI disrupts. Reflective assignments (formulaic and unverifiable) and care-plan coursework.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Prioritisation under pressureIn a simulated handover with four patients and too little time, students sequence actions, escalate appropriately and justify their choices.
    2. Reflection anchored to observed practiceReflection must reference a specific, dated, mentor-corroborated incident and is discussed in supervision.
    3. Patient-facing translationStudents turn a clinical guideline into an explanation for a patient with stated literacy and language needs, then deliver it in simulation.
    Where to secure the evidence Practice-based assessment by practice educators, simulation OSCEs and professional conversations across placements.
  • Veterinary science Certifies: Clinical reasoning across species under resource and owner constraint, practical competence, and handling conflicting obligations.

    What AI disrupts. Written case work-ups and knowledge assessment. Veterinary decisions are constrained by owner finances and welfare at once, which models handle blandly.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. The constrained caseWith a limited owner budget and an incomplete diagnostic pathway, students sequence investigations, decide when to treat empirically, and justify the welfare trade-off.
    2. Herd- or practice-level dataStudents analyse real herd health or caseload data and make a management recommendation.
    3. The difficult conversationA simulated consultation on euthanasia, cost or a client refusing recommended treatment.
    4. Portfolio of clinical encountersA portfolio with reflective, evidence-linked commentary across the clinical years, culminating in a synthesis.
    Where to secure the evidence OSCE-style examinations, direct observation of procedural skills, and a final professional conversation drawing on the portfolio.
  • Social work Certifies: Assessing risk and need in complex family systems, ethical reasoning, and relationship-based practice under statutory constraint.

    What AI disrupts. Case-based written assignments and reflective work.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Contradictory-account case analysisWhere the child’s, parent’s and school’s accounts conflict, students identify what further information is needed and justify a course of action. The skill is holding contradiction, not resolving it neatly.
    2. Anti-oppressive practice audit of AI outputStudents critique an AI-generated case assessment for its assumptions about class, family structure, ethnicity and capacity.
    3. Recorded practice observationStudents are recorded in practice and analyse the observation.
    Where to secure the evidence Practice educator assessment on placement, plus a professional conversation on a case the student carried.

Creative, spatial and applied disciplines

  • Architecture and the built environment Certifies: Spatial and material design judgement, integrating site, brief, regulation, structure, environment and inhabitation.

    What AI disrupts. Image generation has transformed visual production, and written rationales are easily generated — though the studio critique remains largely intact.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Site-specific design with documented analysisStudents survey the site themselves, and the design must respond to the conditions they recorded.
    2. Iterative development portfolioThe rejected schemes, and the reasons for abandoning them, are weighted as heavily as the final proposal.
    3. AI ideation, professionally interrogatedStudents use image generation for early ideas, then critique what the images conceal: structure, drainage, fire escape, buildability and cost.
    Where to secure the evidence Studio critique and final review with external practitioners, with the criteria made explicit.
  • Art and design Certifies: Developing and sustaining an independent creative inquiry, with critical awareness of context and material practice.

    What AI disrupts. Generative tools disturb production and the written contextual component, though the studio model absorbs much of the shock.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Process-led assessmentSketchbooks, maquettes, tests and failures are the primary evidence; the outcome is one data point.
    2. Material and making evidenceStudents are assessed on physical outcomes and documented process.
    3. Positioned contextual studyWritten work situates the student’s own practice against named practitioners and explains where it diverges.
    4. Generative tools as subject matterWhere students use AI, their commentary must address authorship, training-data provenance, labour and aesthetic homogenisation.
    Where to secure the evidence Studio crit, exhibition and viva.
  • Music and the performing arts Certifies: Performance, interpretation, ensemble judgement and creative decision-making in real time.

    What AI disrupts. Written analysis, programme notes and increasingly composition. Performance itself is inherently secure.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Interpretive decision commentaryStudents record two contrasting interpretations of a passage and justify the differences with reference to the score, historical practice and their intent.
    2. Rehearsal process documentationStudents are assessed on how they diagnose ensemble problems and the strategies they use to solve them.
    3. Composition with a defended constraintStudents compose for a real constraint — an ensemble’s instrumentation, a venue’s acoustic, a commission — and justify their choices.
    Where to secure the evidence Live performance and viva. The discipline has never had an assurance problem, and has a lesson for others.
  • Geography and environmental science Certifies: Integrating spatial, physical and social evidence to explain place-based phenomena, and reasoning about scale and uncertainty.

    What AI disrupts. Essays, literature-based coursework and some GIS workflows.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Fieldwork-derived analysisStudents interpret data they collected themselves, attending to what their sampling can support.
    2. Scale-mismatch critiqueStudents identify where a policy or AI-generated assessment applies conclusions from one scale illegitimately to another.
    3. Contested local decisionOn a real regional controversy, students advise a named stakeholder, acknowledging what others would contest.
    Where to secure the evidence Field-based practical assessment with in-situ questioning, plus a dissertation viva.
  • Accounting and finance Certifies: Applying technical standards accurately, exercising professional scepticism, and reasoning about ethical exposure.

    What AI disrupts. Computational tasks, standard-application questions and report writing, as the profession automates rapidly.

    Redesigned tasks

    1. Professional scepticism exerciseStudents find a plausible but improper treatment in a set of accounts, evidence it, and draft the query to management.
    2. Audit the automated outputStudents verify generated workings against source documents and standards, documenting every check.
    3. Ethical exposure memoGiven a client-pressure scenario, students set out the ethical issue against the professional code, the options and their consequences.
    Where to secure the evidence Supervised technical assessment aligned with professional body requirements, plus an oral defence of a case analysis.

Two whole programmes

Securing a few points across three years, so everything else can stay open.

  • BA EnglishFour secured points over three years: in-class close reading, an interpretive oral, a dissertation viva and a symposium Q&A. Everything else is open and allows AI, and marking load falls.
  • BEng Civil Engineering (accredited)A supervised fundamentals assessment, lab work with in-lab questioning, a supervised analysis assessment, and design and project vivas. The individual design viva is the keystone: the attributable evidence accreditation needs, while group projects stay tool-rich.

Eleven ways redesign goes wrong

The anti-patterns to check your own plans against.

  1. The relabelled task. A permission issued, nothing redesigned.
  2. Exams everywhere.
  3. Reflection as security theatre.
  4. Process evidence as surveillance.
  5. The unenforceable partial ban.
  6. Novelty without construct.
  7. Authenticity as a security claim.
  8. Detection as evidence.
  9. Solving it at module level.
  10. Automating the judgement. AI doing summative marking.
  11. Policy without design. Talk is cheap (Corbin, Dawson and Liu, 2025).
