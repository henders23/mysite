Activities and tools

AI as environment-builder

Forty EAP activities in which AI builds the environment and students do the thinking. The tutor uses AI to generate the raw material judgement needs: rival summaries, subtly flawed essays, overconfident experts, broken search strategies. Every activity ends in an artefact of judgement.

Download the full portfolio (Word) Includes a tested tutor prompt and four level adaptations for every activity

Please note. These are suggestions and directions for exploration, not settled answers. They draw on published research and practice but have not been peer reviewed. Adapt what you use to your context, and check your institution’s policies before acting on it.

How it works

  • Most activities need no student AI access. The tutor prepares the material; students judge it. Only the experiments and production tasks in Categories 7–8 put AI in students’ hands, and every mark attaches to critique and verification.
  • Tell students what they are looking at. Every artefact says it was made with AI for the activity and that its faults are deliberate. Invented sources are marked as fictional.
  • Check against the tutor key. Every prompt ends by asking for a separate key listing each planted feature and where it is. Check the artefact against it, then run a second-pass check in a fresh chat.
  • Protect students’ work. Before any student’s writing goes into a model, use your institution’s approved tool, remove identifying details and get the student’s agreement.
  • Debrief on judgement, not answers. The learning is students saying what a good summary, claim or source must look like. Most activities take 45–60 minutes; each gives prep and class times.

1. Comparative & evaluative reading

  • E1 Three summaries, one article Students judge three fluent, incompatible summaries of one article and choose the one a real reader needs.
    Where it fits
    Reading-into-writing modules, any discipline.
    Why it works
    Evaluative judgement grows from choosing between plausible rivals and naming the deciding standard (Tai et al., 2018). Three competent summaries force a standard; one good and one bad do not.
    Time
    Prep: First run 30–45 minutes. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 50–60 minutes, with a 10-minute debrief on standards.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Set reading in; three flawed summaries and Foundation versions out, in one call.
    Task brief for students “You may publish one of these summaries in a study guide. For each, name one thing it preserves and one it drops or distorts, citing the original. Choose one, state your standard, and rewrite its weakest sentence.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “You can publish only one summary. For each, write one thing it does well and one thing it gets wrong or leaves out. Show the line in the article that proves it. Choose the best. Say how you decided. Rewrite its weakest sentence.”

    Students produce: Verdict: choice, standard, evidence table, repaired sentence.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): shorter text; A and C; grid; claim-strength bank (may, suggests, is associated with, causes).
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): current set reading.
    • PG/dissertation: disciplinary article; a fourth summary for the wrong audience; register note.
    Tutor prompt: E1 in the prompt library
  • E2 Map the moves Students map the moves in three AI-written introductions to one study, then test the pattern on a real disciplinary introduction.
    Where it fits
    Research writing: postgraduate pre-sessionals; reports, projects, dissertations.
    Why it works
    Research-article introductions typically establish a territory, establish a niche, then occupy it (Swales, 1990). Varied versions make the moves visible; the real text tests the pattern.
    Time
    Prep: First run 30–40 minutes, including finding a real introduction (journal, or a BAWE corpus student text). Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 50–60 minutes.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Three introductions to one fictional study: usual order, alternative order, missing niche.
    Task brief for students “Label every sentence: territory (why the area matters, what is known), niche (the gap or question) or occupying the niche (what this study does). Which introduction lacks a move, and what does the reader lose? Does the unusual order work? Map the real introduction, then write three sentences on your discipline’s pattern and how you will use it.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Label each sentence T (why the topic matters), G (the gap or problem) or S (what this study does). Which introduction has no G? Why is that a problem for the reader? Now label the real introduction from your subject.”

    Students produce: Three move maps, judgement on missing and reordered moves, real-text map, three sentences on the pattern.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): A and C, 80 words each; T/G/S labels; one worked sentence each.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): their genre (lab report, essay introduction); a real student text.
    • PG/dissertation: two target-journal introductions, then their own draft’s weakest move.
    Tutor prompt: E2 in the prompt library
  • E3 The introduction line-up Students rank three introductions to one essay question against explicit criteria, then rebuild the loser to win.
    Where it fits
    Academic writing at all levels; assessed-essay preparation.
    Why it works
    An argument needs a position, coherently presented (Wingate, 2012); students learn quality by evaluating exemplars (Sadler, 1989); rebuilding adds generation to comparison (Chi and Wylie, 2014, constructive mode).
    Time
    Prep: First run 25–35 minutes. Re-run: 5–10 minutes. Class: 45–60 minutes.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Three introductions to the current question: no map; no position; scope too wide.
    Task brief for students “Rank these introductions for a 1,500-word essay on: an arguable position, a followable map, a scope the word count can honour. Which criterion did you weight most, and why? Rebuild the weakest to rank first, changing at most three sentences.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Which introduction is best for a 1,500-word essay? Ask: Does it give a clear opinion? Does it tell the reader the order of the essay? Is the topic small enough for 1,500 words? Rank the three. Improve the weakest. Change no more than three sentences.”

    Students produce: Weighted ranking; rebuilt introduction, changes underlined.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): two introductions; criteria defined with examples.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): the live module question; the rebuild is a first draft.
    • PG/dissertation: disciplinary genre and metalanguage; link to the E2 map.
    Tutor prompt: E3 in the prompt library
  • E4 Same source, two writers Two paragraphs quote one source accurately and reach different conclusions; students judge which is faithful.
    Where it fits
    Source use; reading into writing; integrity beyond plagiarism.
    Why it works
    Good source use represents what a source claims, not only avoids copied words; accurate quotation can still misrepresent (Pecorari, 2013).
    Time
    Prep: First run 20–30 minutes. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 45–50 minutes.
    Tutor prep with AI
    A fictional hedged source; one faithful paragraph; one framing accurate quotes to overclaim.
    Task brief for students “Both writers quote the extract accurately. Which use is faithful? For each paragraph, state the writer’s claim, the source’s claim, and the gap. Write one sentence of guidance fixing the unfaithful use without abandoning its argument.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Both writers quote the same text correctly. Which writer is fair to the source? For each paragraph, write what the writer says, what the source really says, and the difference. Then write one sentence of advice for the unfair writer.”

    Students produce: Two-column fidelity analysis; one repair sentence.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): source claim pre-paraphrased; one paragraph; E1 bank.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): a real module extract; check a paragraph of their own assignment.
    • PG/dissertation: a third paragraph drops the counter-finding: faithful, inflated, selective.
    Tutor prompt: E4 in the prompt library
  • E5 The paragraph beauty contest Students judge rival paragraphs on one point for topic sentence, evidence and cohesion, then vote with reasons.
    Where it fits
    Core academic writing, foundation to in-sessional.
    Why it works
    Standards become usable when applied to exemplars side by side (Sadler, 1989); transitions such as therefore and however signal logical relations, so cohesion is earned only when signal matches logic (Hyland, 2019).
    Time
    Prep: First run 25–35 minutes. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 45–60 minutes, including cross-group defence of votes.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Three paragraphs: no topic sentence; thin evidence; decorative linkers. Invented studies marked fictional.
    Task brief for students “One paragraph enters the model-answer bank. Judge each on: a topic sentence doing real work; specific, integrated evidence; earned cohesion. Pick the winner, name the best single repair for each loser, and defend your vote against another group.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Choose the best paragraph. Check: Does the first sentence give the main point? Is the evidence specific (names, numbers, results)? Do the linking words (however, therefore) match what the sentences really do? For each other paragraph, write the one change that would help most.”

    Students produce: Winner with warrant; one repair per loser.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): worked example of each standard; two paragraphs.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): a current-assignment claim; one repair for their own paragraph.
    • PG/dissertation: disciplinary genre; a fourth, excellent but off-topic paragraph.
    Tutor prompt: E5 in the prompt library

2. Diagnostic & repair: academic writing

  • E6 The essay with a limp A fluent essay that fails structurally: students diagnose before repairing, and leave with a warning label for model answers.
    Where it fits
    Academic writing; feedback literacy before the first assessed essay; exam strands where model answers circulate.
    Why it works
    An argument is a position held coherently from introduction to conclusion, so structural faults are argument faults (Wingate, 2012). Diagnosing before changing rehearses the judgement needed to act on feedback (Carless and Boud, 2018).
    Time
    Prep: First run 40–50 minutes: faults often land too obviously or faintly. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 60 minutes; Foundation 75 minutes or two sessions.
    Tutor prep with AI
    A 600-word essay, three structural faults. Exam variant: a 400-word “model answer” to a familiar exam task with one factual, one logical and one task-response fault.
    Task brief for students “AI wrote this essay and we planted three structural faults. Name, locate and explain each fault’s damage to the argument: the diagnosis is marked. Write a repair plan, not a repaired essay, ranking repairs by how much each would raise the mark. End with a two-sentence warning label for anyone trusting an unchecked model answer.” Exam variant opening: “AI generated this ‘model answer’, and we planted three faults: one factual, one logical, one in how it answers the task. The machine may have added faults of its own.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “AI wrote this essay. We put three problems in its structure. Find each problem. Say where it is and why it makes the argument weaker. Which would you fix first, second and third, and why? Write two sentences of advice for a student who finds a ‘model answer’ online.”

    Students produce: Diagnosis table, ranked repair plan, warning label.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): 400 words; two faults; fault-type menu.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): their module question; the plan becomes a checklist for their draft.
    • PG/dissertation: disciplinary genre; a fourth fault (a shifting definition); no count, so state confidence; execute one repair.
    Tutor prompt: E6 in the prompt library
  • E7 Cohesion surgery Students strip a paragraph’s cosmetic linkers, map the real logical relations, and re-link so words match logic.
    Where it fits
    Cohesion and coherence; a diagnostic before extended writing.
    Why it works
    Transitions signal how one step relates to the next: addition, comparison, consequence (Hyland, 2019). A wrong transition is a false statement about the logic, not a vocabulary slip.
    Time
    Prep: First run 20–30 minutes: check it makes sense without connectives. Re-run: 5 minutes. Class: 40–50 minutes.
    Tutor prep with AI
    A sensibly ordered paragraph with every connective wrong or decorative.
    Task brief for students “Strip every linking word. For each sentence pair, name the true relation (cause, contrast, addition, example, concession), then choose a connective for it, or none. For two choices, explain what the original linker falsely promised: this matters more than the rewrite.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Delete all the linking words. For each pair of sentences, decide the relationship: cause, contrast, addition, example or concession. Choose a linking word that shows it, or use none. For two of your choices, explain why the original word was wrong.”

    Students produce: Relation map, re-linked paragraph, two-item commentary.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): relation options per gap, two example connectives each.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): then re-link a paragraph of their own.
    • PG/dissertation: two paragraphs, one to re-order; two links lexical (repetition, reference).
    Tutor prompt: E7 in the prompt library
  • E8 The overclaiming literature review Students audit a literature-review extract against honest source notes and downgrade its language until it is true.
    Where it fits
    Dissertation preparation; literature reviews; postgraduate writing. Foundation uses two abstracts of one study.
    Why it works
    Hedges let writers present claims with the precision the evidence warrants (Hyland, 1998); auditing others’ calibration trains the reading that precedes calibrating one’s own.
    Time
    Prep: First run 35–45 minutes. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 60 minutes; Foundation 45 minutes.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Five fictional source notes, an overclaiming review paragraph and the Foundation set, in one call.
    Task brief for students “Using the honest notes on each cited study, decide whether the evidence supports each claim fully, partly or not at all. Rewrite so every claim fits its evidence, marking disagreement as contested. List your downgrades: each is a finding.” Foundation brief (B1–B2): “Here is one study and two abstracts of it. Compare each abstract with the study, sentence by sentence. Mark each sentence: says more than the study shows, says less, or says exactly what it shows. Which abstract would you accept for a student conference? Write three lines telling the other writer what to fix.”

    Students produce: Claim-audit table, calibrated rewrite, downgrade list; Foundation: fidelity table and note.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): two-abstract version; four sentences highlighted; hedge bank (suggests, may, appears to, in this sample).
    • Pre-sessional: as written; three sources if new to reviews.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): three sources on a module topic; rewrite one of their own sentences.
    • PG/dissertation: real field abstracts; one claim to strengthen.
    Tutor prompt: E8 in the prompt library
  • E9 Reference forensics Students find and fix realistic citation errors in a text and reference list, and state the rule each breaks.
    Where it fits
    Any referencing strand; induction; dissertations. Pairs with E35 (do references exist?).
    Why it works
    Citation lets readers trace and trust sources, so it is source use, not formatting (Pecorari, 2013). Ranking errors by severity shifts attention to that purpose.
    Time
    Prep: First run 35–45 minutes: models add unplanted reference errors; check every entry. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 45–60 minutes.
    Tutor prep with AI
    A fictional cited paragraph and reference list with eight seeded errors.
    Task brief for students “The sources are fictional; the errors are real. Find every referencing error (six to eleven), fix it, and state the rule it breaks. Rank them from ‘costs credibility’ to ‘merely cosmetic’, and defend which most damages the writer’s trustworthiness.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Find the referencing mistakes. There are between six and eleven. Correct each one and write the rule it breaks. Which mistake is the most serious for a reader? Why?”

    Students produce: Corrected text and list, error log, severity ranking.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): five errors from a type menu.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): their department’s style; check five of their own entries.
    • PG/dissertation: disciplinary style (APA, a Harvard variant, IEEE); one correct “error” tests overcorrection.
    Tutor prompt: E9 in the prompt library
  • E10 The hedging transplant Students calibrate an over-assertive and an under-assertive text to the evidence, then audit their own draft.
    Where it fits
    Stance and claim strength; postgraduate writing, where overcaution rivals overclaiming. After E27.
    Why it works
    Hedges and boosters signal writers’ commitment to claims (Hyland, 2019); experts fit commitment to evidence and readers (Hyland, 1998). Over- and underclaiming both misstate the evidence.
    Time
    Prep: First run 20–30 minutes. Re-run: 5 minutes. PG: plus 10 minutes per draft. Class: 50–60 minutes, including self-audit.
    Tutor prep with AI
    One results discussion, unhedged and over-hedged. PG tier: the model rewrites a paragraph of each student’s draft at both extremes. Data and consent: approved tool; remove names and identifying details; get each student’s agreement first.
    Task brief for students “Version A believes everything; Version B believes nothing; the evidence (note at the top) deserves neither. Write Version C, calibrated claim by claim. List your five hedging or boosting moves and when each earns its place. Then mark each claim in a paragraph of your draft as calibrated, overconfident or underconfident, and rewrite the worst.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Version A is too sure. Version B is not sure enough. Read the note about the evidence. Write Version C, which is as sure as the evidence allows. List the five words or phrases you used. Then check one paragraph of your own writing in the same way.”

    Students produce: Version C, five-move repertoire, one-paragraph self-audit.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): Version A only; E27 or a hedge bank.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): A and B, then self-audit of the current assignment.
    • PG/dissertation: extremes from their own paragraph (see data note); disciplinary norms of confidence; a 300-word article with three over- and two underconfident claims.
    Tutor prompt: E10 in the prompt library

3. Adversarial & dialogic: seminars & speaking

  • E11 Steelman the position you hate Students present the strongest honest case for a position they reject, marked on whether its real defenders would accept it.
    Where it fits
    Seminar skills; debate preparation; critical thinking through speaking.
    Why it works
    Evaluative judgement means judging an argument’s quality independently of agreeing with it (Tai et al., 2018); building the case you reject forces that separation.
    Time
    Prep: First run 40–60 minutes, mostly checking one real source per position. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 60 minutes for 16: parallel groups of four, four-minute turns with audience marking, then debrief.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Contested positions with case sketches; the model names no studies or authorities. Add one checked real source each, or none and use module reading.
    Task brief for students “Choose the position you most disagree with. Build its strongest honest case, including its defenders’ own concessions, and present it for four minutes as if you held it. The audience marks whether a real defender would sign your version. Strawmen score zero.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Choose the position you disagree with most. Prepare the best case for it: the best evidence and reasons. Present it for two minutes as if you believe it. The audience decides: would a real supporter agree with your version?”

    Students produce: Presentation; a paragraph on what building the case changed in your view.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): short checked evidence packs; two minutes; frame card (“Supporters of this view argue that…”).
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): current seminar topic; three minutes in threes.
    • PG/dissertation: live disciplinary controversies, own sources; two audience questions answered in role.
    Tutor prompt: E11 in the prompt library
  • E12 Cross-examine the expert The tutor projects an AI “expert” defending a confident, flawed position; students cross-examine it live, marked on question quality.
    Where it fits
    Seminar and speaking; question formulation for lectures and supervisions. Tutor runs it live.
    Why it works
    Working with AI means deciding how far to trust outputs whose workings are hidden; questioning tests that trust (Bearman and Ajjawi, 2023).
    Time
    Prep: First run 30–40 minutes, including a rehearsal with likely questions. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 50 minutes: 15 planning, 15 questioning, 20 diagnosis and debrief.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Opening, role instructions and key from a preparation chat; paste role and opening into a fresh chat in class. Model and settings: models may concede early or drop role: rehearse on the live model and tier in a temporary chat, and tighten the instructions if it folds.
    Task brief for students “This AI expert is confident, fluent and wrong in two ways. Your team has eight questions: pin the claim down, expose its basis, then test the weak points. Afterwards, name both flaws, cite the revealing answers, and identify the wasted question that let the expert perform instead of commit.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “The AI expert sounds sure, but its argument has two problems. Your team can ask eight questions. First, make it say exactly what it claims; next, ask what the claim is based on; last, test the weak points. Name the two problems and the answers that showed them. Which question did not help?”

    Students produce: Question plan, two-flaw diagnosis citing answers, wasted-question reflection.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): question stems (“What evidence…?”, “How would you respond to…?”); one flaw.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): a claim from module reading.
    • PG/dissertation: disciplinary flaws (method, scope); no question answerable by definition.
    Tutor prompt: E12 in the prompt library
  • E13 Disagree well A seminar discussion with a real dissenter and five practised interaction moves, heard back in students’ own voices.
    Where it fits
    Any seminar strand; especially where students keep consensus or rarely take the floor.
    Why it works
    Authentic dissent stimulates better group thinking than an assigned devil’s advocate (Nemeth, Brown and Rogers, 2001), so the dissenter argues the objection they find most credible. In exploratory talk, speakers challenge and build on ideas with reasons (Mercer, 2000); its language can be taught.
    Time
    Prep: First run 25–35 minutes. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 50–60 minutes: 10 model exchange, 20 discussion, 20 tally, listen-back and rewrite.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Case card, five-move phrase card, model exchange. Phone recordings only with everyone’s agreement, deleted after the session.
    Task brief for students “Label the moves in the model exchange, then discuss the case. When the group converges, the dissenter makes the objection they find most credible, with a reason. Everyone practises: build on, ask for clarification, challenge with a reason, concede, invite someone in. The observer tallies moves with exact words and records whether the dissent was absorbed or out-argued. Rewrite one of your turns with a move you did not use.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Practise five ways to take part: add to an idea, ask what someone means, disagree with a reason, accept a good point, ask a quiet person to speak. The dissenter says the strongest objection they really believe. The observer writes down who says what. Listen to one thing you said and say it again better.”

    Students produce: Discussion minute (consensus, dissent, its fate), move tally, rewritten turns.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): one move each; phrase card; 10 minutes.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): a seminar-reading case; two 12-minute rounds, observers swapping.
    • PG/dissertation: two conflicting dissenters, ranked; tally compared with disciplinary seminar norms.
    Tutor prompt: E13 in the prompt library
  • E14 Defend your draft A viva in miniature: students answer challenges to their own draft, marked on honest, precise defence, including when to concede.
    Where it fits
    In-sessional writing support; dissertation and extended-essay preparation; presentations.
    Why it works
    Explaining one’s own choices is evaluative judgement applied to one’s own work (Tai et al., 2018); feedback literacy includes managing the feelings a challenge provokes and judging which criticism to accept (Carless and Boud, 2018).
    Time
    Prep: 10–15 minutes per draft, checking the misreadings; one shared anonymised draft, 15 minutes in all. Class: 50–60 minutes for 16: groups of four, six-minute defences, parallel rounds.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Six fair challenges and two misreadings per draft. Data and consent: approved tool; remove names and identifying details; get each student’s agreement first, including to peer challenge; offer a tutor-only defence instead.
    Task brief for students “Defend your draft for six minutes. Some challenges are fair; at least one misreads your text. Concede specifically where a challenge lands; correct misreadings politely, citing your text. You are marked on calibration: defending the defensible, conceding the indefensible, and knowing which is which.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “You will answer questions about your draft. Some are fair. At least one gets your text wrong. If a question is fair, accept the point clearly. If it is wrong, say so politely and show the sentence in your text. You get marks for knowing which is which.”

    Students produce: The defence; a note of two concessions and one evidenced rebuttal.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): three questions in advance; phrase card (“That’s a fair point; I will…”, “I think that’s not quite what I wrote — in paragraph 2 I say…”).
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): volunteers defend one shared draft; all apply the six question types to their own in writing.
    • PG/dissertation: a peer panel challenges cold and marks against disciplinary viva conventions.
    Tutor prompt: E14 in the prompt library
  • E15 Two chairs Students argue each side of a controversy with different evidence, then adjudicate from a third chair what each side owed.
    Where it fits
    Seminars; speaking assessment; controversy-rich modules.
    Why it works
    Judging a dispute fairly is evaluative judgement, sharper after arguing both sides (Tai et al., 2018); the three-chair rotation is a design principle.
    Time
    Prep: First run 40–60 minutes, mostly checking real sources. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 50–60 minutes: threes rotate through all chairs (four, four, three minutes), then debrief.
    Tutor prep with AI
    A balanced controversy pack; the model names no studies or authorities. Add checked real sources, or none and use module reading.
    Task brief for students “Chair one: argue for, four minutes, best case only. Chair two: argue against, four minutes, with different evidence, not a negation. Chair three: adjudicate as a fair reader, naming each side’s best point and the concession it owed. The verdict carries the marks.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “First, argue for the position for two minutes. Then argue against it, with different evidence. Then be the judge: say the best point on each side, and one thing each side should have admitted. The judge’s decision gets the marks.”

    Students produce: Written adjudication: best point and missing concession per side; verdict.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): checked evidence packs; two minutes per chair; frames (“The strongest point for… was…”).
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): a controversy from current reading; one round plus written adjudication.
    • PG/dissertation: current disciplinary debate, own sources; cite a standard the discipline uses to settle such disputes.
    Tutor prompt: E15 in the prompt library

4. Listening & lecture literacy

  • E16 Three sets of notes, one lecture Students judge rival notes and an AI summary of a real lecture by their retrieval value a week later, then rebuild their own notes in the winning style.
    Where it fits
    Listening; study-skills induction; pre-sessional lecture programmes; cohorts whose platform auto-generates AI summaries.
    Why it works
    Notes matter as much for later review as for recording, so their worth shows at retrieval (Kiewra, 1985). Retrieval tests put that function at the centre.
    Time
    Prep: First run 45–60 minutes: correct the captions, generate, check planted errors against the recording. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 60 minutes after the lecture.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Three note sets and a platform-style summary (Set D) with three faults, from the transcript.
    Task brief for students “AI made all four documents from today’s lecture; two contain planted errors. Your essay is due in a week. Test each set on three tasks: find the main claim, the second example, the caveat before the conclusion. Rank Sets A–C, name each one’s trade-off, and find the tidiest set’s error. Find Set D’s three departures from the lecture: deletion, upgrade or invention? Rewrite a section of your notes in the best style.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “AI made these notes and this summary from today’s lecture. Your essay is due next week. Use each set of notes to answer three questions about the lecture. Which set helps most? Find the mistake in the neatest set. Find three places where the summary is different from the lecture. Rewrite part of your own notes in the best style.”

    Students produce: Ranked verdict with retrieval evidence, located error, Set D fault log, rebuilt notes.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): 10-minute segment; group retrieval; Sets A–C only.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): this week’s programme lecture; Set D checked against their own notes.
    • PG/dissertation: no Set D fault count, so confidence counts; test the summary tool they actually use.
    Tutor prompt: E16 in the prompt library
  • E17 The signpost hunt Students predict a lecture’s structure from its discourse markers alone, then listen to test the predictions.
    Where it fits
    Listening at all levels; especially early in a pre-sessional.
    Why it works
    Lecturers organise as well as inform and evaluate, and discourse-organising language signals structure (Deroey and Taverniers, 2011); committing and checking predictions is the planning-and-monitoring cycle of metacognitive listening (Goh and Vandergrift, 2022).
    Time
    Prep: First run 30–40 minutes. Re-run: 5 minutes. Class: 45–50 minutes.
    Tutor prep with AI
    A signposts-only skeleton of an authentic extract. Scripted fallback: a script with two false starts, an aside, a self-correction and “what I want you to notice here is…”, recorded in your own voice rather than a synthetic one where you can.
    Task brief for students “You have only the lecture’s signposts. Predict in writing: how many main points, where the pivot comes, what follows ‘having said that’. Listen and score yourself: where the structure surprised you, was the signpost misleading or did you misread it? Add three entries to your signpost glossary.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “This sheet shows only the lecture’s signposts, like ‘firstly’ and ‘having said that’. Guess: how many main points? Where does the lecturer change direction? Write your guesses. Listen and check. Where were you wrong, and why? Add three signposts to your own list.”

    Students produce: Predictions, scored comparison, three glossary entries.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): signposts pre-taught; predictions from options; shorter extract, played twice.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): a lecture they will attend this term.
    • PG/dissertation: a research seminar or conference talk, with subtler signposting; add prosody (emphasis, pause).
    Tutor prompt: E17 in the prompt library
  • E18 Gist first, detail second Students commit to a gist after one hearing, then reconcile it with details from the second, revising understanding in real time.
    Where it fits
    Listening; exam listening reframed as an academic skill.
    Why it works
    Skilled listeners monitor understanding and revise it when new information strains it (Goh and Vandergrift, 2022).
    Time
    Prep: First run 30–45 minutes, mostly finding a talk whose second half complicates its first. Re-run: 5 minutes. Class: 40–50 minutes for a five-to-eight-minute talk.
    Tutor prep with AI
    An authentic two-movement talk; the model writes gist options and the key. Scripted fallback: a 600-word script of the same shape with natural spoken features, recorded by a colleague.
    Task brief for students “After one hearing, write the speaker’s point in one sentence. On the second, collect the three details that most strain it. Revise your gist to fit them, and write two lines: what did your first sentence assume that the talk never said?” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Listen once. Write the speaker’s main point in one sentence. Listen again. Write down three details that do not fit your sentence. Change your sentence so it fits. In two lines, explain what you changed and why.”

    Students produce: First gist, straining details, revised gist, two-line account.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): gist from four options; details flagged by timestamp.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): a talk in their subject; the account guides next week’s listening.
    • PG/dissertation: a disciplinary conference talk; compare the revised gist with the speaker’s abstract.
    Tutor prompt: E18 in the prompt library
  • E19 What the transcript tidied away Students listen to a real lecture while reading an AI-tidied transcript, catch what the clean-up removed, and decide which spoken features carried meaning.
    Where it fits
    Listening at all levels; lecture-capture induction; cohorts relying on transcripts, captions or AI summaries.
    Why it works
    Natural speech carries hesitations, false starts, repetition and rhythm that listeners must handle (Field, 2008). Much of a lecture’s evaluating and organising — what matters, what is a caveat, what can be skipped — happens in exactly these moments (Deroey and Taverniers, 2011).
    Time
    Prep: First run 30–40 minutes: a six-to-eight-minute extract, corrected captions, key checked against audio. Re-run: 5 minutes. Class: 50 minutes: two hearings, classification, verdict.
    Tutor prep with AI
    A tool-style “readable” version of the corrected captions. Recording used with agreement or under an open licence.
    Task brief for students “AI tidied this transcript of the recording. Listen with it in front of you and mark everything it leaves out. Classify each as noise (filler, false start) or meaning (stress on a key word, ‘what I want you to notice is…’, ‘you don’t need to worry about this’, a correction, a caveat in an aside). What would a reader of the tidy version miss, and which three spoken signals will you now listen for?” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “AI made this clean text from the lecture. Listen and read at the same time. Mark every place where the lecturer says something that is not in the text. Was it just ‘um’ or a repeated word, or did it tell you something important? What would you miss if you only read the clean text?”

    Students produce: Marked transcript, noise-or-meaning classification, three signals.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): three-minute extract played three times; removal count given; type menu with audio examples.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): this week’s module lecture.
    • PG/dissertation: a seminar or conference Q&A, where evaluation hides in asides; mark stress and judge what meaning the transcript lost.
    Tutor prompt: E19 in the prompt library
  • E20 Reconstruct the argument From notes alone, students rebuild a lecture’s argument, then audit against the transcript what their listening kept, mutated and lost.
    Where it fits
    Listening into writing; from note-taking to essay-building.
    Why it works
    Evaluating one’s listening against the text and planning what to change completes the metacognitive listening cycle (Goh and Vandergrift, 2022).
    Time
    Prep: First run 30–40 minutes, mostly checking the model reconstruction. Re-run: 5 minutes. Class: The lecture; 30–40 minutes’ reconstruction (in class, or as homework 48 hours later); 40 minutes’ audit and debrief.
    Tutor prep with AI
    A lecture with a clear argumentative spine and its transcript; template and model reconstruction as key.
    Task brief for students “Forty-eight hours after the lecture, using only your notes, complete the template: claim, grounds, order of moves, concessions. Open the transcript: what survived, mutated or vanished? What will you do differently next week, given what your listening lost?” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Use only your notes. Fill in the template: the main claim, the reasons, the order of the points, and any limits the lecturer admitted. Then read the transcript. What did you keep? What changed? What did you lose? What will you do differently next time?”

    Students produce: Reconstruction, self-audit, one listening adjustment.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): at 24 hours, in pairs; template carries section headings.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): one session: 15-minute extract, 20-minute unrelated task, reconstruction and audit.
    • PG/dissertation: compare two reconstructions before the transcript: where they differ, someone’s listening intervened.
    Tutor prompt: E20 in the prompt library

5. Research & source use

  • E21 The source line-up Five real sources compete to be cited for one claim; students weight criteria before ranking and must catch the journal that fails venue checks.
    Where it fits
    Research skills; library sessions turned into judgement work; ideally co-taught with a subject librarian.
    Why it works
    Evidence quality is judged relative to purpose (Tai et al., 2018), and authority depends on context and information need (ACRL, 2016, “Authority is constructed and contextual”). Real sources mean real checks.
    Time
    Prep: First run 45–60 minutes, mostly finding the sources, best done with your library. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 50–60 minutes, with internet access for the venue checks.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Five real sources: empirical study, review, quality press, practitioner blog, and a paper from a journal your library has documented as failing venue checks (describe it so in class; don’t label it publicly). The model writes only neutral profiles from what you paste.

First commit to weighted criteria: authority, recency, method, independence, fit. One journal fails standard venue checks: find it with the Directory of Open Access Journals and the Think. Check. Submit. checklist, not the writing style, and name the check. Honestly: which source would you have used before today?” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “These five sources are real. Choose the best one for your essay. First, decide what matters most: who wrote it, how new it is, how the study was done, whether it is independent, and how well it fits. Then rank the sources. One journal fails the standard checks: use the checklist to find it.”

    Students produce: Weighted criteria, defended ranking, the venue and the check that caught it, a one-line honesty note.

    Level adaptation

    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): a claim from the current assignment.
    • PG/dissertation: disciplinary sources; for a different purpose (background, not claim support), the ranking must change: explain why.
    Tutor prompt: E21 in the prompt library
  • E22 Paraphrase or patchwriting? Students judge a line-up of borderline rewrites of one source sentence, locating the real boundary between paraphrase, patchwriting and plagiarism.
    Where it fits
    Academic integrity; source use; induction.
    Why it works
    Patchwriting — staying close to a source’s wording and structure — is common in second-language academic writing and often not meant to deceive, so it is better taught as a stage of learning than treated only as an offence (Pecorari, 2003).
    Time
    Prep: First run 20–30 minutes: check each rewrite sits where it should. Re-run: 5 minutes. Class: 45–50 minutes.
    Tutor prep with AI
    One dense, real, referenced sentence; six rewrites across the spectrum, in random order.
    Task brief for students “AI wrote these six versions of one source sentence; only some use it acceptably. Judge each: acceptable, patchwriting or plagiarism, and name the deciding feature: wording, structure, attribution or accuracy. One is well distanced but distorts the meaning: acceptability includes fidelity. Draw your red line in one sentence and compare it with the institution’s.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Here are six versions of one sentence from a source. For each, decide: acceptable, too close to the source (patchwriting), or plagiarism. Say why: the words, the sentence structure, the citation or the meaning. Write your own rule in one sentence and compare it with the university’s rule.”

    Students produce: Judged cases with deciding features; a personal red line checked against the integrity policy.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): three cases; judgements from defined options.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): a sentence from module reading; then check one sentence of their own assignment.
    • PG/dissertation: technical disciplinary sources, where shared phrasing complicates the wording test; note what cannot be paraphrased away.
    Tutor prompt: E22 in the prompt library
  • E23 Five studies, one paragraph Students turn five source notes into one synthesis paragraph organised by ideas, not sources: the anti-annotated-list exercise.
    Where it fits
    Dissertation preparation; literature reviews; postgraduate writing. Follows E8.
    Why it works
    An argument is the writer’s position built from evaluated sources and presented coherently (Wingate, 2012); a synthesis is that argument in miniature, citing sources because the position needs them.
    Time
    Prep: First run 25–35 minutes. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 50–60 minutes.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Five fictional notes that relate (two agree, one dissents on method, one peripheral, one reframes) and a stapled-summaries failure exemplar.
    Task brief for students “The example is the stapled list a literature review must not be. Write the paragraph it fails to be: organised by ideas, agreements grouped, the dissent weighed, the peripheral study put to work or left out (a citation is not a courtesy). Then note, for each citation, the job it does.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “The example paragraph just lists the studies one by one. Write a better paragraph. Put studies that agree together. Explain why one study disagrees. Decide whether the less relevant study belongs at all. For each study you cite, write one line saying why it is there.”

    Students produce: Synthesis paragraph with citation-job notes; the peripheral study’s fate defended.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): three notes; organising-idea menu; phrases for agreement and contrast.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): a topic from the current assignment; swap in real studies and the paragraph enters their draft.
    • PG/dissertation: real abstracts from the field; a second paragraph reorganises the same studies around a different question.
    Tutor prompt: E23 in the prompt library
  • E24 The search-strategy audit Students audit a methodical-looking but broken database search, then run an improved version and report what the original could never have found.
    Where it fits
    Research skills, ideally with the library; dissertation induction.
    Why it works
    Searching is iterative and strategic: choosing sources and terms deliberately, refining as results come in, and knowing what a search leaves out (ACRL, 2016, “Searching as strategic exploration”).
    Time
    Prep: First run 25–35 minutes, plus a test run of the improved search. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 60 minutes with database access; Foundation 40 minutes, no live run.
    Tutor prep with AI
    A realistic flawed search log: synonym gaps, a misused operator, unjustified language and date limits, a premature stop.
    Task brief for students “AI wrote this search log. It looks methodical and is quietly broken. What would it systematically miss, and why? Check the terms (missing synonyms, spellings), operators (what did that AND do?), limits (who vanishes when you filter to English post-2015?) and stopping rule. Rebuild it, run it, and report what the new search found that the old one never could.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “This search has problems. Check the search words: which other words or spellings are missing? Check AND, OR and NOT: are they used correctly? Check the limits on language and dates: what do they remove? Check when the searcher stopped. Write a better search.”

    Students produce: Audit log, rebuilt strategy with a rationale per change, found-versus-missed comparison.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): checklist-guided audit; no live run.
    • Pre-sessional: as written, on a shared question.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): a current-assignment question, in the database their subject librarian recommends.
    • PG/dissertation: their own dissertation question; what will even the improved search miss, and how would a methods section say so?
    Tutor prompt: E24 in the prompt library
  • E25 Quote, paraphrase, or leave it out? For eight source snippets, students make and defend the integration decision — quote, paraphrase, summarise or omit — that referencing guides assume but never teach.
    Where it fits
    Source use and writing; the decision layer above citation mechanics.
    Why it works

    Time

Quote only when the wording itself carries weight; a statistic is not an ornament; leaving out is a decision, not a failure. 6. Calibration & academic caution

  • E26 Supported, unsupported, undeterminable Students classify twelve claims about a text as supported, unsupported or undeterminable, and learn that the third category is where academic caution lives.
    Where it fits
    Reading; critical-thinking foundations; reading exams raised to judgement.
    Why it works
    Design principle: the familiar True/False/Not Given format, raised from exam technique to warranted judgement by requiring a pointer to the text, or its absence, for every verdict.
    Time
    Prep: First run 25–35 minutes: check every category, especially inferences, which models make too loose or tight. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 40–50 minutes.
    Tutor prep with AI
    A real 350-word module extract where possible; twelve claims: four supported (two by inference), four contradicted, four plausible but undeterminable.
    Task brief for students “Judge each AI-written claim from this text alone: supported, unsupported or undeterminable, pointing to the text or its absence. The trap is the third category: ‘sounds right’ is not ‘the text says so’. Undeterminables score double, because academic readers blur that boundary most.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “For each sentence, decide: the text says this (supported), the text says the opposite (unsupported), or the text does not tell us (undeterminable). Show the line in the text, or say there is no line. Careful: some sentences sound true, but the text does not say them.”

    Students produce: Twelve warranted verdicts; the undeterminable boundary in their own words.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): nine claims; inferences flagged; pair discussion.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): current module reading.
    • PG/dissertation: a disciplinary source; four claims needing outside knowledge, with a rule for declaring it.
    Tutor prompt: E26 in the prompt library
  • E27 The stance bank Students sort research-style sentences by the form and strength of their stance devices, check them in real disciplinary writing, and build a usable bank of hedges and boosters.
    Where it fits
    Language focus at all levels; students whose hedging stops at may and might. Precedes E10.
    Why it works
    Hedging is realised through modal verbs, lexical verbs, adverbs, adjectives and nouns, not only modals (Hyland, 1998); hedges and boosters together set a writer’s commitment (Hyland, 2019). Checking real usage rather than trusting the model’s is a design principle.
    Time
    Prep: First run 30–40 minutes, including corpus set-up. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 60 minutes with corpus; 45 without.
    Tutor prep with AI
    30 sentences across five forms and three strengths, plus three stacked combinations. Corpus: BAWE via Sketch Engine, or ten module articles in a free concordancer such as AntConc.
    Task brief for students “Each AI-written sentence has one word or phrase showing how sure the writer is. Underline it; name its form (modal verb, lexical verb, adverb, adjective, noun); say whether it softens or strengthens; place it on a line from ‘tentative’ to ‘certain’. Check five devices in real disciplinary writing: frequency and neighbours (may well, strongly suggests, the possibility that). Bank ten devices, each with a real example and strength note.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Each sentence has a word that shows how sure the writer is. Underline it. Is it a verb, an adverb, an adjective or a noun? Does it make the claim weaker or stronger? Put the words on a line from ‘not sure’ to ‘very sure’. Choose ten words for your own list, with one example each.”

    Students produce: Form-by-strength table, strength line, corpus findings, ten-item stance bank.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): 15 sentences, modals and adverbs; line pre-drawn; a printed tutor concordance of two devices, no live corpus.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): ten module articles as corpus; add two devices to their draft.
    • PG/dissertation: compare disciplines, or target journal with BAWE; which stacked combinations (might possibly) do experts use?
    Tutor prompt: E27 in the prompt library
  • E28 How sure are you? Students rate confidence before checking answers, then track the gap between feeling sure and being right, session after session.
    Where it fits
    Any strand; a recurring ritual around reading or language tasks.
    Why it works
    Evaluative judgement includes knowing the quality of one’s own responses (Tai et al., 2018); committing confidence before checking makes it measurable.
    Time
    Prep: First run 20–30 minutes. Later sessions: 15 minutes. Class: 20 minutes per session, within a longer lesson.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Ten checkable items from current content, a key, and a cumulative record sheet.
    Task brief for students “Answer ten items, committing a confidence beside each from 50% (coin flip) to 100% (certain). Check. Your gap is average confidence minus percentage correct: positive means overconfident, negative underconfident. Log it; after three sessions, plot whether your 90%s are right nine times in ten. Write a one-line rule for next time you feel 90% sure.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Answer the ten questions. For each, say how sure you are: sure, think so, or guessing. Check your answers. Were you right when you felt sure? Were you wrong when you felt sure? Write one rule for next time.”

    Students produce: Answers with confidences, gap score, cumulative record, one rule; after 30+ items, a calibration plot.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): three bands (sure / think so / guessing), counting hits per band.
    • Pre-sessional: as written, over three or more sessions.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): one session: gap score and most overconfident item type.
    • PG/dissertation: disciplinary items; pooled class blind spots and what they imply for the module.
    Tutor prompt: E28 in the prompt library
  • E29 The overgeneralisation hunt Students hunt quantifier and scope errors, and their timid opposites, in a fluent text, repair each claim to fit its evidence, then audit their own draft.
    Where it fits
    Writing and reading; before argumentative essays and critical reviews.
    Why it works
    Calibrated claims fit wording to evidence in both directions: claiming more than the evidence licenses, or less than it supports, both fail precision (Hyland, 1998).
    Time
    Prep: First run 30–40 minutes: check each type, and that the defensible claim is defensible. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 50–60 minutes, including self-audit.
    Tutor prep with AI
    An opinion piece with five overgeneralisation types, two underclaims and one defensible generalisation.
    Task brief for students “AI wrote this text and we planted the problems: the evidence is a footprint, the claims a continent. Find, classify and repair at least eight overgeneralisations: shrink each claim to fit, or state when it would stand. Strengthen the two claims hedged into invisibility. Leave the one defensible generalisation alone. Repairs must still be worth reading. Then audit a paragraph of your own draft.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Some sentences say too much: ‘all’, ‘never’, ‘proves’, ‘everyone agrees’. Find them. Rewrite each so it says only what the evidence shows. Two sentences say too little: make them stronger. One general statement is fine: do not change it. Then check one paragraph of your own writing.”

    Students produce: Hunt log, lively repairs, the defensible claim untouched, a two-line finding from their draft.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): five overgeneralisations, no underclaims; types pre-taught; E27 bank.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): the current assignment topic and draft.
    • PG/dissertation: disciplinary calibration norms; the audit states its norm.
    Tutor prompt: E29 in the prompt library
  • E30 Right for the wrong reasons Students grade the argument, not the answer, in a text with a sound conclusion and broken reasoning: the judgement examiners and supervisors apply.
    Where it fits
    Critical thinking; postgraduate preparation; wherever agreeing with a conclusion passes for accepting an argument.
    Why it works
    Design principle: an argument is judged by whether its grounds support its conclusion, not by prior agreement. A believed conclusion removes the usual cue that something is wrong.
    Time
    Prep: First run 25–35 minutes: models make the false premise too obvious. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 50–60 minutes.
    Tutor prep with AI

    Task brief for students “AI wrote this text and we planted three reasoning faults. You agree with the conclusion: that is the trap. Find the false premise, the statistic doing no logical work, and the passage assuming what it claims to prove. Rebuild the case on legitimate grounds. Then write: where in your recent work did a conclusion you believed excuse an unchecked argument?” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “You probably agree with the conclusion of this text. But the reasons have three problems: one reason is false, one number is not relevant, and one part just repeats the conclusion as if it were proof. Find them. Then write better reasons for the same conclusion.”

    Students produce: Three-fault diagnosis, rebuilt argument, one-paragraph self-application.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): two faults, types illustrated first.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): a module conclusion, so the rebuild uses known reading.
    • PG/dissertation: a disciplinary consensus conclusion; rebuilds graded blind by another group.

7. Generative-then-critical

  • E31 Mark the machine Students grade an AI-written essay against the module’s real criteria, warrant every band with quoted evidence, and compare their marks with the tutor’s.

    Why it works

Band every criterion and quote evidence for each band: ‘it feels like a B’ is not marking. Compare with the tutor’s marks and explain every gap over one band: what did you see or weight differently? The rubric is the course’s language of quality; this is how you become fluent in it.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Mark this essay with your course’s marking criteria.     Level adaptation

    • Pre-sessional: as written.

    • PG/dissertation: programme criteria; a second essay with another profile; justify the rank order.
    Tutor prompt: E31 in the prompt library
  • E32 The delta is the work Students may start from an AI draft, but only tracked, justified improvements are assessed: the delta is the visible work.
    Where it fits
    Writing under AI-permitted policies; the honest alternative to prohibition. Students use AI.
    Why it works
    In AI Assessment Scale terms, students may use AI but are assessed on how they critically evaluate and revise its output (Perkins et al., 2024; Roe, Perkins and Tregubova, 2026). The archived draft and annotations evidence process; detection scores do not, and detectors have misclassified non-native English writing as AI-generated (Liang et al., 2023).
    Time
    Prep: First run 30–40 minutes: run the task prompt yourself; that sample and key calibrate your marking. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 20-minute launch; one to two weeks’ revision outside class; 30-minute debrief.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Task, delta rules and annotation key. Students use the approved tool and record the model.
    Task brief for students “You may generate a first draft with AI; it earns nothing. Your mark is the delta: every tracked change, annotated as accuracy, argument, evidence, register or cut. Ten excellent changes beat a hundred cosmetic ones. End with a judgement summary: your three most important changes, and where the draft showed this tool cannot be trusted.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “You may ask AI to write a first draft. Save it without changing it. Improve it with tracked changes. For each important change, write a letter (A, R, E, G or C) and one line explaining why. Good changes get marks; small cosmetic changes do not. At the end, write the three most important changes and what you learned about the AI.”

    Students produce: Archived draft, annotated revision, judgement summary naming the tool’s failure pattern.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): 300-word draft; eight changes across three types; annotation frames.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): one session: a tutor-supplied AI draft of a 300-word section; five annotated changes.
    • PG/dissertation: identify and defend the section that needed rewriting, not repair.
    Tutor prompt: E32 in the prompt library
  • E33 Three feedbacks, one paragraph Students get three rival AI feedbacks on their own paragraph — vague, overwhelming, confidently wrong — decide what to act on, act, and log uptake.
    Where it fits
    Straight after any marked draft; in-sessional writing support; cohorts already using AI feedback.
    Why it works
    Feedback literacy is making sense of feedback and using it: appreciating it, judging it, managing the feelings it provokes, and acting (Carless and Boud, 2018). Rival feedback forces the judgement students skip when feedback arrives as instructions.
    Time
    Prep: 10 minutes per paragraph, checking the wrong correction is wrong; one shared anonymised paragraph, 20 minutes. Class: 50–60 minutes.
    Tutor prep with AI
    One 150–200-word paragraph per student; three feedbacks each. Data and consent: approved tool; remove names and identifying details; get each student’s agreement first; offer a shared anonymised paragraph instead.
    Task brief for students “AI wrote all three feedbacks, and we asked it to get one point wrong. For every point decide: act, ignore or check first, with a reason. Find the wrong correction and show why your original was right. Revise using only your chosen points, logging each change. Finish: what made feedback worth acting on?” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “AI gave three kinds of feedback on your paragraph. One piece of advice is wrong. For each point, decide: use it, ignore it, or check it first. Find the wrong advice. Improve your paragraph and list each change. Which feedback helped most, and why?”

    Students produce: Sorted feedback table, the wrong correction, revised paragraph with uptake log, two lines on usable feedback.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): shared anonymised paragraph; A and C only; act / ignore / check cards.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): current assignment; add the tutor’s feedback as a labelled fourth version to compare.
    • PG/dissertation: students write their own feedback prompt in the approved tool, judge its output, and compare with supervisor feedback.
    Tutor prompt: E33 in the prompt library
  • E34 The prompt ladder Students climb from a lazy prompt to a precise one over four documented rungs: prompt quality is task understanding, and the top rung is knowing what to verify.
    Where it fits
    AI literacy in writing or research strands; study skills. Students use AI.
    Why it works
    Design principle: a prompt is a task specification, so improving one exposes what the writer understands about the task. Working with AI includes learning what it must be told and what in its output still needs checking (Bearman and Ajjawi, 2023).
    Time
    Prep: First run 30 minutes: climb the ladder once yourself. Re-run: 5 minutes. Class: 60 minutes.
    Tutor prep with AI
    A genuine task (feedback on a draft introduction) and the protocol. Data and consent: students paste their own drafts: approved tool, names and identifying details removed. Model and settings: temporary chat, memory off; record model and version.
    Task brief for students “Rung one is the prompt everyone writes: ‘improve my introduction’. Run it, diagnose the failure precisely (generic praise? wrong register? invented content?), and let the diagnosis write rung two. Climb four rungs. Submit the ladder and an analysis: what did the machine need to be told, and what does that reveal about the task? Top rung: what would you still verify, and how?” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Ask the AI: ‘Improve my introduction.’ What is wrong with its answer? Write it down. Use that to write a better prompt. Do this three times. At the end, write what the AI needed to know, and what you still need to check in its final answer.”

    Students produce: Four-rung ladder, task analysis, verification list.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): three rungs; diagnosis prompts (“What did it praise that doesn’t deserve it?”); shared anonymised introduction.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): the current assignment; three rungs in 45 minutes.
    • PG/dissertation: disciplinary task; a peer tests the final prompt on another text for portability.
    Tutor prompt: E34 in the prompt library
  • E35 Reference-check the machine Students verify every reference in an AI-written paragraph, hunting the invented, mangled and misattributed, and write a four-step protocol for life.
    Where it fits
    Research and integrity; the most transferable AI-literacy exercise here. Pairs with E9 (format) and E36 (a rigged text).
    Why it works
    Trust in AI output should rest on checking, because its workings cannot be inspected (Bearman and Ajjawi, 2023); evaluating AI output is a core dimension of AI literacy (Ng et al., 2021). Unlike E36, the faults are the model’s own.
    Time
    Prep: First run 45–60 minutes: hand-verifying six references and their claims takes 20–40 minutes alone. Re-run: 15 minutes (models change: re-verify). Class: 50–60 minutes, with library and database access.
    Tutor prep with AI
    A six-reference paragraph cited from memory; verify each yourself for the key. Model and settings: many assistants search the web by default and return real references: switch search off, choose a niche topic, record model and version. If every reference checks out, that is the finding: only verification could show it.
    Task brief for students “AI wrote this paragraph from memory, checking nothing; every reference looks real. Verify all six: does the source exist, do its details match, does it say what is claimed? Classify each: verified, mangled (real work, wrong details), invented, or misattributed (real work, wrong claim). Then write your permanent protocol for any machine-suggested source: four steps or fewer, fast enough to use.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “AI wrote this paragraph and its references without checking them. Search for each reference in the library. Does it exist? Are the details right? Does it really say what the paragraph says? Label each one. Write four short steps you will always use to check a reference from AI.”

    Students produce: Six-reference verification log; four-step protocol.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): three references; library search demonstrated first.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): their subject, via the library discovery service they use.
    • PG/dissertation: disciplinary references; ensure one real, correctly cited source does not say what is claimed: the costliest fault to catch.
    Tutor prompt: E35 in the prompt library

8. AI literacy: interrogation & production

  • E36 The hallucination hunt Students hunt planted falsehoods in a fluent, authoritative text with sources open, and learn that confidence is not evidence.
    Where it fits
    AI literacy; reading; induction. Pairs with E35, where the faults are the model’s own.
    Why it works
    Working with AI calls for trust calibrated by checking, not by how confident output sounds (Bearman and Ajjawi, 2023). The text is rigged: it shows what checking feels like, not how often models err.
    Time
    Prep: First run 45–60 minutes: check every sentence meant to be true. Re-run: 15 minutes. Class: 50–60 minutes, sources open.
    Tutor prep with AI
    An encyclopaedic text with five falsehoods of different types; verify the rest.
    Task brief for students “AI wrote this text and we planted five falsehoods; the rest is checked. Hunt with sources open: verification, not suspicion. Log each falsehood, its type, the disproving source and any tell. Which survived longest, and what does its camouflage teach you about falsehoods without a key?” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “There are five false statements in this text. Use reliable sources to find them. For each, write the false statement, what kind of mistake it is, and the source that shows it is false. Which was hardest to find, and why?”

    Students produce: Five-catch verification log; analysis of the hardest catch.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): count and paragraphs given; sources pre-selected.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): a module topic and its sources.
    • PG/dissertation: no count; disciplinary text; one suspicious claim is true: precision matters as much as recall.
    Tutor prompt: E36 in the prompt library
  • E37 The sycophancy test Students test whether an AI holds its position under polite, confident, wrong pushback, and what an agreeable system cannot be used for.
    Where it fits
    AI literacy; a tutor demonstration turned into student experiments. Students use AI.
    Why it works
    Assistants trained on human feedback can abandon correct answers when challenged, and human preference judgements tend to reward agreement (Sharma et al., 2024). Testing it turns a warning into evidence.
    Time
    Prep: First run 40–50 minutes: verify questions; pilot on the students’ model. Re-run: 15 minutes (re-pilot). Class: 60 minutes.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Protocol, questions, pushback scripts, record sheet. Model and settings: behaviour varies by model and tier, and developers have worked to reduce sycophancy, so one “Are you sure?” may barely move simple facts. One model and tier for the class; temporary chats, memory off; single and three-turn pushback; log model and version.
    Task brief for students “Ask, get an answer, push back politely, confidently and wrongly. Does the model hold, fold or hedge? Test facts (folding is failure) and judgements (updating may be right), with one pushback, then three. Log ten trials verbatim. Memo: under what pressure does it abandon correct answers, what does that mean for checking your work, and did you hope it would agree?” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Ask the AI a question. Then tell it, politely, that it is wrong, even though it is right. Does it keep its answer, change it, or become unsure? Try once, then three times in a row. Write down exactly what it says. What does this tell you about using AI to check your work?”

    Students produce: Ten-trial log; findings memo with one implication for their own AI use.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): five trials; supplied questions and scripts; pairs.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): module questions, about a tool they may revise with.
    • PG/dissertation: mirror condition: does it cave to confidently asserted correct answers too, and is that the same flaw?
    Tutor prompt: E37 in the prompt library
  • E38 Same question, three registers Students ask one question in casual, academic and expert registers and analyse what each buys and costs: register is a lever, not decoration.
    Where it fits
    AI literacy in writing; register teaching in a live laboratory. Students use AI.
    Why it works
    Assistants adjust answers to cues about the user and what the user seems to want (Sharma et al., 2024); comparing hedges, boosters and other interactional markers across answers makes this visible (Hyland, 2019).
    Time
    Prep: First run 25–30 minutes: run all three once; complete the key. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: 50–60 minutes.
    Tutor prep with AI
    A module question, protocol and grid. Model and settings: memory and personalisation contaminate the comparison: one temporary or incognito chat per register; record the model.
    Task brief for students “Ask one question casually (‘so basically why does…’), academically, and as one expert to another. On the grid: which claims appear in all three? What evidence and hedging does each register buy? What did the casual answer omit, and what did the expert one assert with unearned confidence? Which register suits study, and what must you re-check regardless?” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “Ask the AI the same question in three ways: casually, in academic English, and like an expert. Compare the answers on the grid. Which ideas appear in all three? Which gives more evidence? Which is too sure? Which way of asking is best for study?”

    Students produce: Comparison grid, register verdict, one register-induced risk.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): scripted registers; two grid dimensions; a fourth, first-language condition compared with English.
    • Pre-sessional: as written; first-language condition optional.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): a current-assignment question.
    • PG/dissertation: disciplinary metalanguage: did terminology buy accuracy or only its costume?
    Tutor prompt: E38 in the prompt library
  • E39 The glossary that must not lie Students build an AI-drafted glossary of course terms and verify every element, because next year’s students will trust it.
    Where it fits
    Any strand; end-of-course consolidation for a real audience. AI use optional.
    Why it works
    Evaluating AI output against authoritative sources is one dimension of AI literacy, handling its ethical issues another (Ng et al., 2021); publishing to real readers makes producers answerable for both. The real audience is a design principle.
    Time
    Prep: First run 30–40 minutes: terms, standard, key. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: Two 60-minute sessions (drafting and verification; peer review and sign-off), with verification between.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Term list (or groups mine it) and standard. Agree publication with the next cohort’s tutor.
    Task brief for students “Draft with AI if you wish, then verify every element of every entry against an authoritative source: course text, subject dictionary, cited study. A soothing but misleading definition is worse than jargon. Log every draft error: the error museum is half the value. Next year’s students will trust this. Sign your entries and state in one line how you used AI.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “You may use AI to write first definitions of the course’s key words. Check every part with a reliable source, such as the course book or a subject dictionary, and write down the source. If the AI was wrong, record the mistake. Next year’s students will use your glossary, so it must be correct. Put your name on your entries.”

    Students produce: Verified, signed glossary; source log; AI-use line; error museum.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): eight terms; sources supplied.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): five module terms in one 60-minute session, published on the module’s learning-environment page.
    • PG/dissertation: two contested terms: represent the contest, don’t resolve it falsely.
    Tutor prompt: E39 in the prompt library
  • E40 The exam board The capstone: teams produce an AI-drafted, student-calibrated mock assessment; another group sits it; producers mark and defend their standards in moderation.
    Where it fits
    Any strand with several weeks; the closing activity. Students use AI.
    Why it works
    Setting, exemplifying and defending standards is evaluative judgement at full stretch (Tai et al., 2018), giving the assessor-side evaluative experience Sadler (1989) argues students need. Defending marks in moderation rehearses the judgement that makes feedback usable (Carless and Boud, 2018).
    Time
    Prep: First run 30–40 minutes: standard, calibration checklist, moderation protocol. Re-run: 10 minutes. Class: Two to three weeks: 30-minute launch; production outside class; 45-minute sitting; 60 minutes’ marking and moderation.
    Tutor prep with AI
    Package standard and calibration rule: model answers must earn their intended bands under the team’s criteria.
    Task brief for students “Produce the package: a task worth setting; defensible criteria; a strong and a deliberately mid-level model answer, each earning its intended band under your criteria (we check); and an examiner’s report predicting candidates’ errors. Draft with AI if you wish, and say how. Another group sits it; you mark their scripts and defend contested marks in moderation.” Plain-English version (B1–B2): “In your team, make a practice test: a task, marking criteria, one strong and one average model answer, and a report on the mistakes students will probably make. You may use AI. Another group takes your test. You mark their answers and explain your marks.”

    Students produce: Calibrated package with AI-use statement; marked scripts; moderation defence.

    Level adaptation
    • Foundation/pathway (B1–B2): task stem and criteria skeleton; one model answer.
    • Pre-sessional: as written.
    • In-sessional UG (one-off workshop): a 90-minute mini board: one task, two criteria, one model answer, sat by the next team.
    • PG/dissertation: a third group audits the package against the learning outcomes as external examiner.
    Tutor prompt: E40 in the prompt library
