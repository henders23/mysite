Authorship in the age of AI

A working definition of authorship for assessment, and eight principles for designing around it.

The question

What counts as a student's own work now, and how do I explain it?

The difficulty is less technological than conceptual. Scholarly publishing has already settled it: an author is someone who can answer for the work (ICMJE, 2025; COPE, 2023). This framework brings that standard into student assessment.

The definition

For the purpose of student assessment, authorship is accountable, reflective and dialogic meaning-making: the student's demonstrable capacity to take responsibility for, explain and defend the meaning of their work, including any use of AI, within the conventions of their discipline.
  • Accountable The author can explain, defend and correct the work, and answer for its claims. It's not about who typed the words.
  • Dialogic Writing has always woven in other people's words (Bakhtin, 1981). AI is one more voice. The author shapes borrowed material on purpose, and answers for it.
  • Reflective Judgement can't be seen in a finished product, but it shows in process and dialogue. The capability to build is evaluative judgement (Tai et al., 2018).
The rule to teach You may hand over control, but you may not hand over responsibility. After Eaton (2023) and Bearman and Ajjawi (2023)
The question to ask of any task In this genre, where does the author's accountable judgement reside?

Eight principles

For teachers and course designers.

  1. 01 Design for authorship. Don’t try to detect it. Detector output is at most a prompt for a conversation, never evidence of misconduct (Weber-Wulff et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2023).
  2. 02 Make accountability the standard. Students stand behind their meaning, orally or in writing. They may hand over control, but not responsibility (Eaton, 2023; Bearman and Ajjawi, 2023).
  3. 03 Use a two-lane, programme-level design. A few secured points such as vivas, interactive orals and supervised tasks. Most tasks open and rich in process (Bridgeman, Liu and Weeks, 2024; Dawson, 2021).
  4. 04 Set permitted AI use per task, and teach disclosure. Use a shared scale, and treat honest, specific AI-use statements as part of the work (Perkins et al., 2024; Roe, Perkins and Tregubova, 2024).
  5. 05 Assess process and judgement, not only product. Commentaries, annotated drafts, portfolios, vivas and interactive orals. Ask whether the task makes the student answer for the work (Tai et al., 2018; Sotiriadou et al., 2020).
  6. 06 Teach source use and voice, and treat AI as one more voice. Fold AI into existing teaching on citation, paraphrase, stance and voice, with students marking what it contributed (Hyland, 2005; Pecorari, 2003; Ivanič, 1998).
  7. 07 Lead with equity. Test every design for whom it disadvantages. Keep secured assessment small, provide tools institutionally, and protect multilingual students (Liang et al., 2023; Warschauer et al., 2023).
  8. 08 Embed, don’t bolt on. Authorship teaching belongs inside disciplinary curricula, built in partnership between academic literacies practitioners and discipline staff.

Across the disciplines

Where accountable judgement lives in nine common genres.

  • Natural sciences Laboratory report Weight interpretation, error analysis and reasoning from this experiment’s data. A short oral account of an unexpected result is the secured check.
  • Engineering Design report Assess the justification of design choices and trade-offs, defended at a design review. AI may suggest alternatives; the student explains why theirs won.
  • Computing Code Evidence understanding, not production: explain, modify and debug live. Code walkthroughs and vivas are the secured checks (Prather et al., 2024).
  • Law Legal reasoning and writing Apply law to new facts and defend the reasoning orally, as in a moot. AI research is allowed only if every authority is verified and disclosed.
  • Medicine and health Reflection and case write-up Weight clinical reasoning and reflection on the specific encounter, ideally discussed with a supervisor who was there.
  • Humanities Essay and close reading Close reading of set passages, writing linked to seminars, and a voice that can be traced across drafts. AI can be an opponent to argue against.
  • Social sciences Research report Weight the student’s own analysis of the data. AI may map literature if the student shows what it got wrong or missed.
  • Creative arts and design Portfolio and process The portfolio, documented process and artist’s statement already evidence authorship. AI is one medium among others, disclosed.
  • Business and management Report and case analysis Use live, local or bespoke cases, defended in a presentation with questions as the secured element.

Use it tomorrow

One task. Five decisions.

  1. 1Ask where, in this genre, the student's accountable judgement lives. That's what to assess.
  2. 2Set the permitted AI use for this task, using a shared scale, and tell students why.
  3. 3Teach students to write a specific AI-use statement, and treat it as part of the work, not a confession.
  4. 4Add one moment where the student answers for the work: a short oral explanation, an annotated draft or a reflective commentary.
  5. 5Ask who this design disadvantages, and adjust until the answer is acceptable.

Where it falls short

  • The fixes carry their own risks. Too many invigilated exams narrow what's assessed. Surveillance erodes trust. Process evidence adds workload and penalises non-linear writers. Orals can disadvantage anxious, multilingual and disabled students.
  • The evidence on disclosure is thin. AI-use statements can turn into a compliance ritual unless they're taught and valued.
  • Open use could widen gaps. Some groups use AI far more than others (Freeman, 2025). Without teaching and institutionally provided tools, open tasks could widen attainment gaps.

What would change this position

The position should be revisited if any of these is shown. None has been reached so far.

  • Detectors are independently shown to have low false-positive rates, with no demographic bias.
  • AI-use statements are shown to be systematically gamed in ways teaching can't fix.
  • Open use is shown to widen attainment gaps even with provided tools and teaching.
