The short answer

Generative AI can improve learning or damage it. The strongest studies suggest the difference lies less in the tool than in the design around it: what the AI is set up to do, what the student still has to do unaided, and how learning is measured.

The evidence base is young and uneven. Most studies are short, run in a single setting, and measure performance soon after the intervention. Treat every finding below as provisional. This page covers research published to November 2026.

Does AI help or harm learning?

  • What the strongest studies foundThe largest meta-analysis, of 69 experimental studies, mostly in university language teaching, reported gains in academic performance and motivation (Deng et al., 2025). But two critical reviews of its primary studies found that few support causal claims: most AI and control conditions differed in more than the technology – in teaching method, practice with the test or time on task – or gave too little detail to tell (Weidlich et al., 2025; Lawson et al., 2026). A second widely cited meta-analysis (Wang and Fan, 2025) was retracted in 2026 over discrepancies in its analysis.
  • How confident we can beLow. There is no trustworthy overall effect size for ‘AI and learning’, and the question may be too broad to have one. Better studies ask which design, for which students, measured how.
  • What it means for teachingBe sceptical of any headline that says AI improves, or destroys, learning. Ask what the AI did, what the comparison group did, and whether students were tested without AI.

Tutoring and feedback

  • What the strongest studies foundIn a field experiment with about 1,000 high-school maths students in Turkey, unrestricted GPT-4 raised practice scores by 48% but left students scoring 17% lower than controls on a later exam without AI. A tutor version, prompted to give hints rather than answers, raised practice scores by 127% with no exam penalty (Bastani et al., 2025). In a randomised crossover trial with 194 Harvard undergraduates on an introductory physics course, an AI tutor built on the same pedagogy as the active-learning class produced larger post-test gains, in less time, than the class itself (Kestin et al., 2025). In a six-week EAP study with 48 English-as-a-new-language learners, GPT-4 essay feedback produced the same learning outcomes as feedback from a human tutor (Escalante, Pack and Barrett, 2023).
  • How confident we can beModerate for the direction, low for the size. These are strong designs, but each covers one setting and short exposure. Kestin et al. tested two lessons with an immediate post-test and did not measure retention; Bastani et al. studied school pupils, not undergraduates.
  • What it means for teachingGuardrails decide the outcome. If you deploy an AI tutor, write its instructions yourself: withhold answers, ask one question at a time, and give hints only after effort. This is the Workshop zone of the Threshold Model. Then check what students can do with the AI switched off.

Writing and cognitive offloading

  • What the strongest studies foundIn a lab experiment with 117 university students, those revising with ChatGPT improved their essays most, but gained no more knowledge, and transferred no more, than students supported by a human expert, a checklist or nothing; they also showed fewer metacognitive processes (Fan et al., 2025). In the widely reported ‘Your brain on ChatGPT’ study, 54 participants wrote essays over three sessions with an LLM, a search engine or no tool while their brain activity was recorded by EEG. The LLM group showed the weakest brain connectivity, reported the lowest ownership of their essays and struggled to quote their own work (Kosmyna et al., 2025).
  • How confident we can beLow to moderate. Kosmyna et al. is a preprint, revised in December 2025 and not peer-reviewed, with a small sample (only 18 completed the fourth session), one task and a lab setting; brain connectivity is not the same thing as learning. But the direction is consistent across studies: AI improves the product faster than it improves the learner.
  • What it means for teachingDon’t read a better text as better learning. Assess the process as well as the product, and protect a few difficulties in each module where students work without AI: the Threshold Model’s protected difficulties.

Detection

  • What the strongest studies foundSeveral widely used detectors consistently misclassified non-native English writing as AI-generated, while native writers’ samples were classified accurately (Liang et al., 2023). Later work is more mixed. A detector built for a large-scale writing assessment showed no bias against non-native speakers (Jiang et al., 2024), and one commercial detector achieved near-zero error rates in a 2025 working-paper evaluation (Jabarian and Imas, 2025).
  • How confident we can beContested, and changing fast. Liang et al. used a small essay set and 2023-era detectors. Performance varies by tool, text type, length and the writer’s language background, and even a low false-positive rate produces many wrongful flags across thousands of scripts.
  • What it means for teachingA detector score is not evidence on its own. The Office of the Independent Adjudicator’s casework note says decision-makers should understand the strengths and limitations of detection software, consider a range of evidence, and consider whether assumptions could be biased against a student whose first language is not English or who is disabled (OIA, 2025). Check your institution’s policy, and design tasks that leave a trail of process evidence.

What students do and think

  • What the strongest studies foundIn HEPI’s survey of 1,054 full-time UK undergraduates (December 2025), 95% used AI in some way, up from 66% in 2024. 94% used it for assessed work, and 12% included AI-generated text directly, up from 3% in 2024. 68% saw AI skills as essential, but only 48% felt teaching staff were helping them develop those skills (HEPI, 2026). Jisc’s 2025 discussion groups (173 FE and HE students at five institutions) and partner surveys found employability the biggest worry. Some students avoid AI altogether, fearing any use counts as cheating. Students want clear, course-specific rules, practical training, fair access, and human feedback and discussion kept central (Jisc, 2025).
  • How confident we can beHigh for how widespread use is, lower for what students actually do. These are self-reports, and use in assessed work may be understated.
  • What it means for teachingAssume use. Replace generic policy with course-specific guidance in plain language, and teach the skills students say they want: verifying outputs, using AI ethically, and knowing what data is safe to share.

Equity

  • What the strongest studies foundUse is uneven. Men remain more frequent users than women, and students from more advantaged backgrounds are more likely to use some tools. HEPI notes that the cause of the socioeconomic gap is unclear, since free versions exist (HEPI, 2026). Students say paid tools give some peers an advantage, while disabled and neurodivergent students report particular benefits (Jisc, 2025). Only 38% say their institution provides AI tools, though this is up from 9% in 2024 (HEPI, 2026). Detection adds a further risk for multilingual writers (Liang et al., 2023).
  • How confident we can beModerate for the gaps, low for their causes. We know the gaps exist; we don’t yet know what drives them, or how they affect attainment.
  • What it means for teachingProvide access through the institution where you can, teach AI use explicitly rather than assuming it, and keep integrity processes off tools with known bias risks. Include AI-dependent tasks in your equality impact checks.

How to read a new study

Before you act on a study, check four things: the sample (who, how many, and how like your students), the setting (lab, school or a real course), the outcome measure (learning tested without AI and after a delay, or only the quality of work produced with it) and the guardrails (what the AI was set up to do, and what the comparison group got). A study that is vague on any of these cannot tell you much.
