A ready-to-run AI induction session for students

A 75-minute session, adaptable to 60 or 90, that teaches new students what generative AI is, the rules they work under, and how to check, declare and protect their own learning.

For
Induction leads, personal tutors, academic skills and EAP staff
You’ll get
Aims, a slide-by-slide outline, three activities and a run sheet
Time
10-minute read
Last reviewed
November 2026

Please note. These are suggestions and directions for exploration, not settled answers. They draw on published research and practice but have not been peer reviewed. Adapt what you use to your context, and check your institution’s policies before acting on it.

What this session assumes

You aren’t introducing AI. In a December 2025 survey of 1,054 full-time UK undergraduates, 95% reported using AI in some way and 94% used it to help with assessed work. Fewer than half (48%) felt teaching staff were helping them develop AI skills (Stephenson and Armstrong, 2026). Induction is where you set the habits they will use it with.

Students need no AI accounts. You run the one live demonstration on an institution-approved tool, and prepare the activity materials in advance. Review every prepared artefact before class: activities that hinge on planted errors collapse if the errors aren’t there.

The session is neither for nor against AI. It treats critique and refusal as legitimate outcomes, as the site’s AI literacy activities do. Wherever it says [insert], add your institution’s own policy, wording and contacts. Don’t paraphrase rules you haven’t checked.

Aims

By the end, students can:

  • Explain what generative AI does.Say, in plain terms, how a language model produces text and why fluent output can be wrong.
  • Find and read the rules.Locate the institution’s policy and a task’s AI-use statement, and tell a secured task from an open one.
  • Verify.Check a claim, a reference and a quotation against a source.
  • Disclose.Write a specific AI-use declaration, by type of contribution.
  • Protect their own learning.Recognise when not to use AI, because the effort is the learning.
  • Get help.Name where to go with questions about AI use, sources, support needs or an allegation.

The three activities

Each is a shortened version of an activity on this site. The full versions run 45–60 minutes and suit a follow-up session.

  • E37 The sycophancy test (10 minutes, slide 4)

    From the EAP activities. In the full version, students run ten trials and write a findings memo. Here, you run three trials live: two factual questions with verifiable answers in the discipline, and one judgement question.

    Prepare the questions and a pushback script (‘Are you sure? I read that…’). Students predict, then record whether the model holds, folds or hedges. Folding on a fact is failure; updating on a judgement may be reasonable.

    Debrief: under what pressure did it give way, and what does that mean for using it to check your own work? Current models hold their ground more often than earlier ones, so plan for either result.

  • E36 The hallucination hunt (18 minutes, slide 7)

    From the EAP activities, where it is recommended for induction. Before the session, generate an encyclopaedic text on a checkable topic from the programme. Plant five falsehoods of different types: a wrong date, an invented statistic, a misattributed idea, a plausible fake entity, and a true fact given a false cause. Edit them in by hand if the model won’t, and check that everything else is accurate.

    In pairs, with sources open, students log each catch: the falsehood, its type, the source that disproved it, and the tell that made them check.

    Debrief on the falsehood that survived longest. Without an answer key, that is the kind they will miss.

  • Activity 6 The line between editing and ghost-writing (10 minutes, slide 10)

    From the AI literacy activities. In the full version, students use AI for sentence-level feedback on their own draft, log every suggestion, and write a disclosure statement. For induction, prepare a 150-word sample paragraph and six AI suggestions: some good fixes, one that changes the meaning, one that adds an unsupported claim, one that shifts the register.

    Students mark each suggestion accepted, modified or rejected, with a reason. Then they write a three-sentence declaration using the contribution types on slide 9.

    Debrief on where editing becomes ghost-writing. Set the full version as a follow-up on students’ first real draft.

Changing the length or format

  • 60 minutes.Run E37 as a five-minute demonstration with one factual question. Cut E36 to three falsehoods and 12 minutes. Merge slides 8 and 9 and rely on the handout.
  • 90 minutes.Run E36 in full with a longer debrief. Add Activity 11 Detecting the ‘AI voice’ after slide 6, so students see for themselves why judging authorship by style is unreliable.
  • Online.Use breakout rooms for pairs and a shared document for logs. Share the E36 text as a PDF, and ask students to verify with the library and databases, not with AI.
  • Large lecture.Run E36 individually, then poll the room on each suspected falsehood before revealing it.
  • Never cut.The local rules (slides 5–6) and where to get help (slide 12).
