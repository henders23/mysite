Leading AI change in a department

A briefing for the people who commission staff development: the five decisions your department has to make this year, how they usually go wrong, a 12-month plan, and the UK rules that frame it.

For
Heads of department, programme directors and associate deans
You’ll get
A decision table, a 12-month plan and the UK regulatory frame
Time
7-minute read
Last reviewed
November 2026

Please note. These are suggestions and directions for exploration, not settled answers. They draw on published research and practice but have not been peer reviewed. Adapt what you use to your context, and check your institution’s policies before acting on it.

Why this is a leadership problem

Almost every student now uses AI. In HEPI’s 2026 survey of 1,054 full-time UK undergraduates, 95% used it in some way and 94% used it to help with assessed work, but only 48% felt their teaching staff were helping them develop these skills (Stephenson and Armstrong, 2026).

Individual lecturers can’t solve this module by module. Assessment, policy, staff capability and tools interact, and only someone with authority across programmes can make them line up. That is a head of department’s job, not something to delegate to whoever is most interested in AI.

The decisions you have to make this year

Five decisions, the usual options, and what a good outcome looks like by the end of the year.
DecisionOptionsWhat good looks like
AssessmentBan AI in most tasks; permit it with declarations; design at programme level, with a few secured points and open tasks everywhere else.Each programme knows where secured, individual evidence is gathered and why. Every other task states its AI stance and the reason for it.
PolicyAdopt the institutional policy unchanged; write a departmental policy; translate the institutional policy into task-level statements.For any task, a student can say what AI use is acceptable and why. The OIA calls this clarity essential (OIA, 2025).
Staff capabilityOptional webinars; a one-off all-staff day; sequenced development built on the team’s own modules.Every module lead has tested their own assessments against current tools and can explain the stance to students.
Student guidanceGeneric institutional guidance; module handbook statements; AI literacy taught progressively across the years.Students are taught to use AI well in their discipline, and when not to use it. They aren’t just told the rules.
Tools and dataLeave tool choice to individuals; approve a short list of institution-licensed tools; build or buy discipline-specific tools.Staff and students use licensed tools, no student work goes into unapproved ones, and your data protection officer has reviewed any new processing.

Four ways it goes wrong

  • Policy without design.A permission statement changes nothing about what a task actually assesses. Corbin, Dawson and Liu (2025) argue that changes which only tell students what is allowed can’t be enforced, so validity has to be built into the task itself. Change the structure of assessment, not just the instructions attached to it.
  • Module-by-module fixes.When each module lead patches their own assessment, you get duplication (the same outcome examined five times), gaps (programme outcomes evidenced nowhere securely) and contradictory rules for students. Map the programme first.
  • Relying on detection.A detector score looks like evidence, but it isn’t proof. The OIA expects decision-makers to understand the strengths and limitations of detection software, to share all the evidence with the student, and to remember that the provider must prove the case (OIA, 2025). An integrity strategy that rests on a detector produces appeals.
  • One-off training.A single all-staff session raises awareness, and then the effect fades. Capability grows when staff work on their own modules, try ideas with students, and come back to compare what happened.

A 12-month plan

Start this term so that the changes reach next year’s validation and approval deadlines.

  1. 01Months 1–2: name an owner and mapAppoint a lead with time allocated. Map every assessment in each programme: the task, its weighting and timing, whether it is secured, and which programme outcome it evidences. This exposes the gaps and the duplication.
  2. 02Months 2–3: test assessments against current toolsEach module lead runs their own briefs through current AI tools, including agentic tools that can act in a browser, and marks the result. Opinion becomes evidence, and it is the fastest way to build a shared understanding.
  3. 03Months 3–5: agree the programme stanceDecide where the few secured points sit (four to eight across a three-year degree is an illustrative starting point), what AI stance every other task takes, and how secured outcomes count towards the award. Involve your quality team early.
  4. 04Months 4–6: build capability on real workRun sequenced development with time between sessions, using the team’s own modules and briefs. Include marker calibration for any new oral or viva.
  5. 05Months 6–8: redesign and approveRewrite briefs and criteria, draft student-facing AI-use statements, and take the changes through approval in time for next year. Ask your quality team what notice or consent changes need for students already on the course.
  6. 06Months 8–10: guide studentsPublish each task’s stance and the reason for it. Teach AI literacy in induction and inside modules, not only in a handbook.
  7. 07Months 10–12: evaluate and adjustCheck the measures below, brief external examiners, and decide what to scale and what to drop.

How to judge whether it’s working

  • Every task has a stance and a reason.Check a sample of module handbooks and briefs, not the policy document.
  • Every programme outcome is secured somewhere.The map shows no orphans, and no outcome secured over and over.
  • Students can say what is allowed.Ask them through a short survey or the student–staff committee. Don’t infer it from the policy.
  • Misconduct cases rest on better evidence.Fewer cases rely on detector output, and fewer are overturned on appeal.
  • Staff have changed something.Count redesigned briefs approved, not training attendance.
  • Assessment load is stable or lower.Secured points should replace existing assessment, not add to it.

The UK frame

This is the regulatory and sector context, not legal advice. Check the specifics with your quality, registry and legal teams.

  • OfS Condition B4 (England).Providers must ensure that students are assessed effectively, that each assessment is valid and reliable, and that awards are credible. ‘Valid’ means the assessment results in students demonstrating knowledge and skills (OfS, 2022a). B4 doesn’t mention AI, but a task AI can complete no longer shows what the student knows, and that makes it a B4 question. The guidance also says that not taking reasonable steps to prevent and detect plagiarism would likely be of concern. B4 applies to providers registered with the OfS; Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own arrangements.
  • QAA.QAA has not been England’s designated quality body since April 2023 (OfS, 2022b), so in England its AI work is sector guidance, not regulation. It is still worth using: the Quality Compass on the AI era (QAA, 2024), and the 2026 project Assuring Academic Standards in the Age of AI, which is consulting on draft sector principles and shared definitions of assessment terms, including validity (QAA, no date).
  • Russell Group principles.The 2023 principles commit member universities to help students and staff become AI-literate, to adapt teaching and assessment to incorporate the ethical use of AI and support equal access, and to uphold academic rigour and integrity (Russell Group, 2023). They have not been revised since July 2023.
  • The OIA on misconduct.The OIA’s 2025 casework note sets the practical standard: make clear to students what is and isn’t acceptable use, give them all the evidence and a chance to explain how they worked, and remember that the provider carries the burden of proof, on the balance of probability (OIA, 2025).
  • Equality Act duties.If you add orals or vivas, plan alternatives in advance. The EHRC’s note on the Abrahart case says that methods of assessment will rarely, if ever, be competence standards, and that the duty to make reasonable adjustments is anticipatory (EHRC, 2024).

Do this first

Map before you act. A one-page map of where each programme gathers secured evidence will tell you more than a policy review, and it gives your staff development real work to do.
