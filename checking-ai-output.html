Checking AI output: a one-page guide for students

AI tools write fluently and are often right. This page shows you how to check when they’re not, and when not to use them at all.

Please note. These are suggestions and directions for exploration, not settled answers. They draw on published research and practice but have not been peer reviewed. Adapt what you use to your context, and check your institution’s policies before acting on it.

Fluent is not the same as true

AI chatbots produce text by predicting likely words from patterns in their training data. So the writing is smooth and confident whether or not it is correct. A wrong answer reads exactly like a right one. How it sounds tells you nothing about whether it’s true.

Tools that search the web make fewer obvious errors, but they can still misreport what a source says. A real reference attached to a claim it doesn’t support is harder to spot than a fake one. Whatever you submit, you are responsible for it.

How to check each kind of output

Five things AI gives you, and the check for each.
AI gives youHow to check it
Claims and factsFind each claim in a reliable source you can name: a textbook, a peer-reviewed article, an official dataset. If you can find it only in AI output, don’t use it.
ReferencesLook each one up in the library catalogue or a database. Check the authors, year, title and journal match. Then open it and check it says what the AI says it does.
QuotationsFind the exact words in the original, with a page number. If you can’t, don’t present them as a quotation.
NumbersTrace each figure to its original source and date. Redo any calculation yourself or in a spreadsheet. Check the units, and ask whether the result is plausible.
CodeRun it. Test it on cases where you know the answer, including unusual ones. Check every library or function it uses really exists. Don’t submit a line you can’t explain.

When not to use AI

AI can help you finish a task and still stop you learning from it. In a study of nearly 1,000 secondary school maths students, those given an ordinary chatbot did better in practice, but 17% worse than students who never had it once it was taken away. A version that gave hints rather than answers largely avoided the harm (Bastani et al., 2025).

  • When the effort is the point.If a task is there to build a skill, such as writing an argument, solving a problem or reading a hard text, doing it yourself is the learning.
  • When you can’t judge the answer.If you don’t know enough to spot an error, you can’t check the output. Learn the basics first.
  • When the rules say no.Read the AI-use statement for each task. If it’s unclear, ask before you start.
  • When you’d share what isn’t yours to share.Don’t paste in personal data, confidential material or other people’s unpublished work. Use the tools your university approves.
  • Instead: make it question you.Ask it to quiz you, give hints, or argue against your view, rather than do the work for you.

One rule

If you can’t check it, don’t use it. If you can’t explain it, don’t submit it.

My checking checklist

Copy this into your notes, or print it and keep it by your desk. Run through it before any AI output goes into your work.

BEFORE I USE AI OUTPUT IN MY WORK

[ ] I can name a reliable source, other than the AI, for every factual claim.
[ ] I have found every reference in the library catalogue or a database, and checked it says what is claimed.
[ ] I have found every quotation, word for word, in the original, with a page number.
[ ] I have traced every number to its source and redone every calculation.
[ ] I have run and tested any code, and I can explain every line.
[ ] My use fits the AI-use statement for this task.
[ ] I can explain and defend everything I submit without the AI.
[ ] I have saved my prompts and the outputs I used.
[ ] I have completed my AI-use declaration honestly and specifically.
