Supporting multilingual students fairly

Why policing language puts the burden on multilingual students, what the detector evidence now shows, and how to write tasks, policies and feedback that separate language support from content generation.

For
Module leads, policy writers, EAP and academic skills teams
You’ll get
Fair policy wording, a support-or-content table and a design checklist
Time
8-minute read
Last reviewed

Please note. These are suggestions and directions for exploration, not settled answers. They draw on published research and practice but have not been peer reviewed. Adapt what you use to your context, and check your institution’s policies before acting on it.

Start with what you are assessing

Most UK programmes assess knowledge, reasoning and judgement through English, not English itself. When AI policy treats the surface of a text as evidence of who did the thinking, multilingual students carry a burden their peers don’t: polished prose looks suspicious, unpolished prose loses marks, and the help that closes the gap is the help the policy fears.

If marks or suspicion vary with accent, idiom or how native a sentence sounds, that is construct-irrelevant variance (Messick, 1989). The remedy is design: say what each task assesses, permit the language support that doesn’t touch it, and secure judgement in ways that don’t depend on prose style.

There is a legal edge. Language is not a protected characteristic, but race is, and it includes nationality and national origins (Equality Act 2010, ss. 4 and 9). A rule that disadvantages students of particular national origins is indirect discrimination unless it is a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim (Equality Act 2010, s. 19). Ask your equality team to review AI policy with that in mind.

What the detector evidence says

Liang et al. (2023) ran 91 TOEFL essays by non-native writers through seven detectors. The average false-positive rate was just over 61%, while essays by US eighth-graders were classified accurately; 89 of the 91 were flagged by at least one detector. Asking ChatGPT to make the word choices sound more native cut the rate to about 12%, so the detectors rewarded using AI to disguise one’s own English. The authors call it a pilot, with small samples and mostly GPT-2-based detectors.

Later evidence is mixed. Detectors trained on essays from one large-scale writing test showed no bias against non-native speakers in that setting (Jiang et al., 2024). A 2026 preprint on Czech found no systematic bias across three detector families (Al Ali, Helcl and Libovický, 2026). A 2025 working paper reported near-zero false positives for one commercial detector, but tested no student essays or second-language writing (Jabarian and Imas, 2025).

So the bias question is open, and policy should say so. What is not open: no study tests your students’ hybrid drafts, where their own ideas pass through a grammar tool or a translation step; a score is a probability about a text, not proof about a person; and the provider must prove misconduct (OIA, 2025a). In one upheld complaint, an international student who believed a grammar tool was allowed for non-native speakers was penalised after a detector flag and a viva that never asked how they had worked (OIA, 2025b).

How policing language shifts the burden

  • The fluency double bindIf fluent prose is suspicious and errors lose marks, there is no safe way to write. Students leave errors in, or hide help they were entitled to.
  • Proof falls on the studentDrafts and version history protect students only if they kept them and can explain them under pressure in a second language. Ask everyone for process evidence, or it becomes a test only some students sit.
  • The rules are unclear‘Proofreading’ and ‘paraphrasing tool’ mean different things across academic cultures. The OIA asks providers to consider whether a first language other than English affected a student’s understanding of what was permitted (OIA, 2025a).
  • Legitimate help goes undergroundWhen language support is lumped in with cheating, students stop disclosing it and stop asking EAP staff about it, which is where the learning would have happened.

Language support or content generation?

Where the line usually falls, using the contribution taxonomy from this site’s in-sessional EAP guide, with translation added. Set the line for each task, and tell students where it is.
ContributionUsually language supportCrosses into content
Language repairCorrecting grammar, spelling, articles and word forms in sentences the student wroteRewriting paragraphs so that the argument, emphasis or claims change
RegisterMaking the student’s own point more formal, hedged or conciseAdding evaluation, claims or stance the student did not make
TranslationTranslating the student’s own notes or draft from another language, then revising the EnglishTranslating text the student did not write and presenting it as their own
StructuringChecking that the student’s own outline follows the genre’s expected movesProducing the outline or section plan that the student then fills in
IdeationExplaining an unfamiliar term or concept, in plainer English or another languageSupplying the argument, angle or research question
ContentOnly where the task permits AI-generated material and asks for it to be labelledGenerating text, analysis or findings submitted as the student’s own
Source workChecking that a reference list is formatted consistentlyFinding, summarising or citing sources the student has not read
FeedbackAsking for comments on the clarity and accuracy of a draftAccepting rewrites wholesale instead of acting on the comments

Design fair tasks and policies

  1. 01Name the construct in every briefOne sentence on what the task assesses and whether English accuracy is part of it. If it isn’t, say that errors which don’t obscure meaning won’t be penalised.
  2. 02Grant language-repair permission explicitlyUnless language is the construct, permit language repair and register support on text the student wrote. Name tool types, not products, and point to institutionally licensed tools.
  3. 03Ask for disclosure by contributionReplace yes-or-no declarations with the seven categories of the contribution taxonomy in this site’s in-sessional EAP guide: ideation, structuring, language repair, register, content, source work and feedback. Practise it in formative work first. Disclosed, permitted use should never on its own start an investigation.
  4. 04Set the level with the EAP-AIASThe EAP-AIAS adapts the AI Assessment Scale (Perkins et al., 2024) to EAP’s dual aims of language proficiency and academic acculturation. Its five levels run from no AI to full AI (Roe, Perkins and Tregubova, 2026). In the preprint, the middle levels cover AI-assisted language input, limited language or skills practice, and task completion with critical evaluation (Roe, Perkins and Tregubova, 2024). Use it for EAP tasks and to explain departmental briefs.
  5. 05Treat translanguaging as a resourceDrafting notes or first drafts in another language is normal multilingual practice (Canagarajah, 2013). Say whether translating a student’s own writing is permitted; treat it as language support unless the task assesses English production.
  6. 06Make process visible for everyoneIf you collect drafts or a commentary on how the work was made, collect them from every student, with credit attached, not only from those under suspicion.
  7. 07Secure judgement without prose styleWhere you need assurance, use a short conversation about the work, not a detector score, and assess substance. ‘Oral assessment and vivas at scale’ covers the detail.

Policy wording: avoid and use

Six swaps that make a policy clearer and fairer. Adapt them to your institution’s regulations.

  • Avoid: “No AI tools may be used.”It silently bans grammar and translation features built into word processors. Use: “You may use AI tools to correct grammar, spelling and word choice in text you wrote. You may not use them to generate ideas, arguments or text for this task.”
  • Avoid: “Work that appears to be AI-generated will be investigated.”Appearance is not evidence, and fluent writing is not a red flag. Use: “Concerns about unauthorised AI use are handled under the academic misconduct procedure. A detector score or writing style alone will not be treated as evidence.”
  • Avoid: “Proofreading and paraphrasing tools are not allowed.”Both terms are unclear across languages and academic cultures. Use: “You may correct the language of your own sentences. Rewording that changes your argument, or rewording other people’s text, is not allowed.”
  • Avoid: “Did you use AI? Yes / No.”A yes reads as a confession. Use: “Tick each kind of help you used (ideation, structuring, language repair, register, content, source work, feedback) and add one line on how.”
  • Avoid: “Your work must be entirely in your own words.”Ambiguous for students who draft in another language or use standard academic phrasing. Use: “The ideas, analysis and structure must be yours. Common academic phrases and corrected language are fine.”
  • Avoid: “Errors in English will be penalised.”Unless English is the outcome, this marks the wrong thing. Use: “You are not marked on English accuracy unless errors stop your meaning being clear.”

Language is not evidence

Neither fluency nor error tells you who did the thinking. Decide what each task assesses, permit the language support that doesn’t touch it, and gather evidence of judgement in ways that don’t depend on how native the prose sounds.

Feedback that keeps language and content apart

Separate comments on content and reasoning from comments on language, and say which errors matter: those that obscure meaning, not every slip. ‘This reads like AI’ is not feedback; it is an accusation without a procedure.

Where AI language repair is allowed, teach students to treat its suggestions as feedback to evaluate, not text to accept. A short log of changes accepted and rejected turns repair into noticing. Warschauer et al. (2023) set out the affordances and three contradictions of AI text for second-language writers, with a five-part approach: understanding, accessing, prompting, corroborating and incorporating.

What EAP and academic skills teams can offer

  • Policy testingRead draft AI policy and briefs with multilingual students before publication, and rewrite whatever they misread.
  • Disclosure practiceRun the contribution taxonomy in formative tasks, so students meet it before it counts.
  • Brief decodingMap departmental AI statements onto EAP-AIAS levels and explain what each permits.
  • Process coachingShow students how to keep drafts and notes as ordinary good practice, not as a defence.
  • Marker and panel briefingsBrief markers and misconduct panels on detector limits, second-language writing and legitimate language support.
  • A safe place to askOffer consultations where students can discuss specific uses of AI without fear of penalisation, as the Russell Group (2023) principles envisage. Agree confidentiality limits first.
