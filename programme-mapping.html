Mapping AI across a programme

How a programme team charts where AI is taught, used, restricted and assessed in every year, so that a few secured points carry the assurance and everything else can stay open.

Please note. These are suggestions and directions for exploration, not settled answers. They draw on published research and practice but have not been peer reviewed. Adapt what you use to your context, and check your institution’s policies before acting on it.

Why map the whole programme

When each module responds to AI on its own, you get contradictory rules, duplicated exams and programme outcomes that nobody secures. The degree makes its claim at programme level, so that is the level to design at.

Stage 1 of the Assessment redesign map says map before you design. A TESTA-style audit, which looks at assessment patterns across a whole programme rather than module by module, is a good method (Jessop, El Hakim and Gibbs, 2014). This page adds the AI layer to that audit.

Sydney’s two-lane approach makes the same case: secured assessment of learning is planned mainly at program level, and open assessment for and as learning mainly at unit level (Bridgeman, Liu and Weeks, 2024). Treat it as a well-argued design proposition rather than an evaluated intervention.

Four stances for every task

Give every assessed task, and every significant learning activity, one of these stances.

  • Taught.AI use is explicitly taught and practised, with an AI literacy outcome attached. Usually formative.
  • Used.The task is open: students may use AI within stated limits, and must say how they used it. Most tasks belong here.
  • Restricted.Particular uses are excluded for a stated reason. Use this sparingly. Outside supervised conditions you can’t enforce it, so it easily becomes the unenforceable partial ban.
  • Secured.Conditions in which AI can’t contribute, such as supervised, oral or observed assessment, so that the evidence is attributable to the student.

Where the secured points sit

Keep secured evidence sparse. Four to eight points across a three-year degree, each carrying several programme outcomes, is an illustrative starting point; stage 4 of the Assessment redesign map explains why. Progress them from foundational reasoning in year 1 to integrated professional judgement in the final year. If your institution uses the AI Assessment Scale, record each task’s level alongside its stance (Perkins, Roe and Furze, 2025).

The two whole programmes on the Activities and tools page show the pattern. BA English has four secured points over three years: in-class close reading, an interpretive oral, a dissertation viva and a symposium Q&A. Everything else is open and allows AI. BEng Civil Engineering uses a supervised fundamentals assessment, lab work with in-lab questioning, a supervised analysis assessment, and design and project vivas. The individual design viva is the keystone: it supplies the attributable evidence accreditation needs, while group projects stay tool-rich.

Then decide how secured outcomes count towards the award. You could make them a gate on linked modules, concentrate grades in them, or rebalance the classification weighting. Take your choice to your academic regulations committee before validation.

Plan an equally rigorous alternative for every oral or viva in advance. The EHRC’s note on the Abrahart case says that methods of assessment will rarely, if ever, be competence standards, and that the duty to make reasonable adjustments is anticipatory (EHRC, 2024).

Graduate attributes

Most institutions already have graduate attributes such as digital capability, critical thinking or ethical practice. Don’t bolt on a separate ‘AI attribute’. Write the AI dimension into the attributes you already have, and link each one to a final-year outcome that is evidenced at a secured point.

Ask what a graduate of this programme should be able to do with AI, and without it, in their first job or in further study. The second half matters: a graduate who can’t work without the tool hasn’t met an outcome that assumes independent judgement. The Russell Group principles commit member universities to support students to become AI-literate (Russell Group, 2023). The map is how a programme shows that it does.

PSRB constraints

For accredited programmes, the professional, statutory or regulatory body (PSRB) sets limits the map must respect.

  • Read the current standard.Examples include the GMC’s Outcomes for graduates, the NMC’s standards for student supervision and assessment, and the Engineering Council’s AHEP standard for accreditation. Check the current version and any AI statement the body has issued. Don’t rely on memory or a colleague’s summary.
  • Separate the competence from the method.A PSRB may require a competence to be observed. That doesn’t make a particular format a competence standard (EHRC, 2024). If a body appears to require a method, confirm this with them before you refuse an adjustment.
  • Count what is already secured.Placements, observed practice, labs and clinical assessments are often secured already. Map these first, and you may need fewer new secured points than you think.
  • Confirm, don’t assume.Mapping a secured point to an accreditation outcome is a claim to check with your accrediting body or link officer. It doesn’t guarantee accreditation.
