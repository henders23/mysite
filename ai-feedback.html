AI and feedback in practice

How to use AI to multiply practice and formative response while every act of academic judgement stays human: what to hand to AI, what to keep, and how to make students act on what they get.

For
Module leads, tutors and academic skills staff
You’ll get
A feedback loop to run next week, a student prompt and three discipline examples
Time
12-minute read
Last reviewed
November 2026

Please note. These are suggestions and directions for exploration, not settled answers. They draw on published research and practice but have not been peer reviewed. Adapt what you use to your context, and check your institution’s policies before acting on it.

The principle

Feedback has always been rationed by staff time. AI removes the scarcity, but only if you separate the economy of feedback from the economy of judgement. In the Threshold Model, AI sits wholly inside the first. It gives the instant verdicts (F1) and the questioning (F2) of the Workshop, where practice is frequent, low-stakes and ungraded. It sits wholly outside the second: no AI marks, feedback text, ranking or triage at the Bench.

That only works if students act on what they get. Feedback is not information handed over; it is what happens when a student makes sense of it and uses it (Carless and Boud, 2018; Winstone and Carless, 2019). So the thing you design is not the AI comment. It is the loop: practice, AI response, student action, human checkpoint.

What to give AI, and what to keep

Design the loop

Four moves per cycle. Run a cycle every week or fortnight, on one or two criteria at a time.

  1. 01PracticeSet a short, ungraded task tied to named criteria: a 300-word section, three worked problems, one paragraph of analysis. Small pieces get used; whole drafts get skimmed. Keep the module’s two to four protected difficulties, where AI stays off, out of the loop.
  2. 02AI responseStudents run their work through the feedback prompt below, with your criteria pasted in. The AI quotes their text, ties each point to a criterion, and asks rather than rewrites.
  3. 03Student actionStudents revise, then write a short feedback response note: what they changed, what they rejected and why. The rejections matter most. They show judgement about the feedback, not just compliance with it.
  4. 04Human checkpointEach week, read a sample of response notes, say five per seminar group, and open the next class with the patterns you find. Add calibrated peer review against exemplars for a second view (Nicol, 2021). At the Threshold, students bring three to five curated pieces of practice with a rationale. At the Return, they get three feedforward targets and must reply in writing.

Feedback literacy is the real outcome

Carless and Boud (2018) define student feedback literacy as the understandings, capacities and dispositions needed to make sense of feedback and use it. Each of their four features changes when the feedback comes from a machine.

  • Appreciating feedback.Students treat AI comment as one input among several, valued where it is strong and discounted where it is weak. Ask them to compare an AI comment with yours or a peer’s on the same piece.
  • Making judgements.Students weigh each AI point against the criteria and decide whether it is right. The response note makes that judgement visible and assessable.
  • Managing affect.AI feedback is easy to take because it feels private and often flatters. Students need practice with the uncomfortable kind, which is why the prompt must name a weakness.
  • Taking action.Nothing counts until something changes in the work or in the student’s approach. Build the revision into the task, not after it.

Four risks, and how to design against them

  • Sycophancy

    Language models tend to agree with the user and tell people what they want to hear, a behaviour traced partly to training on human preferences (Sharma et al., 2024). Students get praise, or a retraction as soon as they push back.

    Design against it: require at least one unmet criterion in every response, forbid praise that isn’t tied to a criterion, and tell the model to change its view only for a reason. Teach students to distrust feedback that feels good and says nothing.

  • Generic comments

    AI comment can be long, padded and much the same for everyone. In one comparison it contained filler and prioritised less well than trained teachers’ comments (Steiss et al., 2024).

    Design against it: require a quotation from the student’s text for every point, cap the response at three points, and tie each to a named criterion. If a comment would fit any essay, it isn’t feedback.

  • Over-reliance

    When AI makes the work easier, students can stop doing the thinking. In one large school trial, unrestricted chatbot access raised practice scores but lowered later unaided exam scores (Bastani et al., 2025). In another, ChatGPT support raised essay scores without any greater gain in knowledge or transfer (Fan et al., 2025).

    Design against it: no rewriting, ever; questions before observations; AI off for the protected difficulties; and judgement points where students explain their work live.

  • Students not acting on feedback

    This predates AI. Students fail to use feedback when they don’t find it useful, lack strategies for using it, or don’t understand its academic language (Jonsson, 2013). They engage when they can appraise their own work, understand the standards, set goals and stay motivated (Winstone et al., 2017). Unlimited AI feedback widens the gap between receiving and using.

    Design against it: make the response note part of the task, read a sample every week, and treat the Return as incomplete until the student has replied in writing.

What the evidence says about AI feedback quality

Steiss et al. (2024) compared ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) with trained teachers on 200 school history essays from US grades 6 to 12. Teachers were slightly better on four of five quality measures, with the largest gap in prioritising what mattered; ChatGPT was slightly better at criteria-based comment. The teachers were unusually well trained and had more time than most, and the study measured the feedback, not what students did with it.

In a university data science course, GPT-4 feedback on open-ended writing was more readable than instructors’ feedback and contained more of the elements of effective feedback, such as feed-forward and prompts to self-regulate (Dai et al., 2024). Over six weeks with 48 university students of English, those given GPT-4 feedback did as well as those given human tutor feedback, and a second group of 43 who received both were split almost evenly on which they preferred (Escalante, Pack and Barrett, 2023). With 459 upper secondary learners of English, AI feedback improved revisions compared with no feedback, but the effect was small (d = 0.19) (Meyer et al., 2024).

A review of 103 higher education studies found that the quality of AI feedback is usually judged indirectly, through short interventions and students’ perceptions, with teachers mostly in a supervisory role (Çallı and Er, 2026). Few studies test what students can do later, without the tool, and those that do sound a warning (Fan et al., 2025).

The fair reading: current models can produce formative feedback good enough for early drafts, and often better structured than a busy marker manages. The evidence is short term, much of it from schools or single courses and older models, and it rarely measures learning. Treat your own module as the test, and look at what students can do unaided at the judgement points.

Student feedback prompt

Give this to students with the criteria filled in. It works in any general-purpose chatbot. Ask students to use the one your institution has approved, so their work stays within its data agreements, to paste only their own work, and to record the use in their AI-use declaration. Fill in the [square brackets] before use.

You are a feedback coach for [module code and name]. I am a student. Your job is to help me improve my own work against the criteria below. You must not rewrite, correct or add to my text, and you must not give me a mark, a grade or a prediction of one.

The task: [paste the task brief, or summarise it in one sentence]
The criteria I am working on today: [paste one or two criteria, word for word, with the descriptor for the level I am aiming at]
My draft: [paste one section of no more than about 500 words. Remove any names or details that could identify another person.]

How to respond:
1. First ask me what I was trying to do in this section and which part I am least sure about. Wait for my answer before going on.
2. Then give me no more than three observations. Tie each one to a named criterion, quote the exact words from my draft it refers to, and say what the criterion asks for that this passage does not yet do.
3. Turn each observation into a question that helps me see the problem for myself. Do not suggest replacement wording.
4. Name at least one thing that does not yet meet the criteria, even if the draft is strong. Do not praise anything unless you say which criterion it meets and why.
5. If I disagree, judge my reasons on their merits. Change your view only if I give you a good reason, not because I push back.
6. If you are not sure whether something is accurate in my subject, say so and tell me to check it against the module materials or with my tutor. Do not guess.
7. If I ask you to rewrite, write or fix any part of my text, refuse and ask me a question instead.
8. End by asking me to write, in my own words, the one change I will make first and why.

After this conversation I will revise my draft and record in my feedback response note what I changed, what I rejected and why.

Three worked examples

Illustrative designs, not reports of practice. Each keeps the same loop and changes what the AI is asked to look for.

  • Engineering: design justificationPractice: in weeks 2 to 5 of a second-year design module, students write a 300-word justification for one design decision. AI response: it checks that every claim traces to a specification value, a unit or a source (F1), then asks what would change if a load case or constraint changed (F2). Student action: students revise and log in their decision record which question changed the design. Human checkpoint: a week 6 design review in which the tutor changes a requirement live.
  • Law: problem questionsPractice: first-year contract students answer a short problem question each week. AI response: it asks the student to name the issue, the rule and the authority for each step, and flags any authority not on the module reading list without saying whether it exists. Student action: students check every authority in a legal database and record what they checked. Human checkpoint: calibrated peer review against a tutor-marked exemplar in the seminar, then oral advocacy at the judgement point.
  • Nursing: reflective writingPractice: after placement shifts, students write a short reflection using the reflective model the programme teaches. AI response: it asks what the student would do differently and which proficiency the episode bears on; it does not rewrite or judge clinical decisions. Students remove anything that could identify a patient, colleague or placement, and take distressing events to a person, not a chatbot. Human checkpoint: two reflections and their action points, discussed with the personal tutor or practice supervisor.
