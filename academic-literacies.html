Academic literacies and AI

What academic literacies provision is for now, and what it should do, subject by subject, when the texts students read and write are routinely co-produced with AI.

The question

How do I redesign reading and writing activities now students have AI?

AI hasn't created a new literacy so much as made an old set of academic literacies questions unavoidable (Lea and Street, 1998, 2006). Yet that field has been largely absent from the AI literacy frameworks shaping policy. Anson (2024) is one of the first substantial bridges between the two.

In-sessional provision is the best vantage point in the university for this work. It is centrally resourced, delivered into the disciplines, staffed by trained text and genre analysts, and it isn't the assessing party.

Three responses to AI

Without noticing, institutions have reinvented Lea and Street's three models of student writing. They are nested, not alternatives.

  1. AI as study skills Prompt workshops, tool guides and integrity briefings. The deficit sits with the student. Radically insufficient: technique taught out of context doesn't transfer (Wingate, 2006).
  2. AI as socialisation Inducting students into their discipline's norms of AI use. Treats norms as settled when they're contested, and being invented in real time (Lillis, 2001).
  3. AI as academic literacies AI use as situated practice, tied to authority, identity, knowledge and power. Produces better questions rather than tidy rules. The register in-sessional work should mainly operate in.

What models know

Genre knowledge has four dimensions (Tardy, 2009). Models are strong in only one.

What language models have in each dimension of genre knowledge
Dimension What it covers What models have
FormalStructure, register, vocabulary, citation formatA great deal
Subject matterKnowing the field and its live debatesBroad, shallow, unreliable at the edges, and not positioned
ProcessHow texts are drafted, circulated and revisedNone in any meaningful sense
RhetoricalWhat the genre is for, and who reads itNone

The implication is a reallocation. Formal knowledge is now cheap, so teaching time should move to the three dimensions models can't supply.

The surface no longer proves competence. AI has publicly broken the link between "writtenness" and ability (Turner, 2018). That ends the language-repair-shop view of EAP, and makes detectors, which reintroduce the old disadvantage in a worse form, a pressing equity issue (Liang et al., 2023).

Seven dimensions

A working construct for in-sessional provision. Only one of the seven is about the tool.

  1. 1Textual discrimination
  2. 2Genre judgement
  3. 3Epistemic positioning
  4. 4Authorial identity and voice
  5. 5Practice awareness
  6. 6Critical-institutional literacy
  7. 7Communicative agency

The text-analysis protocol

Compare an expert text, a student text and a generated one. About three hours in full; any stage runs in 20 minutes.

Ten principles for materials design

  1. 01 The artefact is the analysis, not the output. Students produce something about text, not polished prose.
  2. 02 Ground truth must be disciplinary and available. Name the field’s checkable authority: a law report, a formulary, a corpus, a primary source.
  3. 03 Design against durable properties, not current behaviour. Label any activity that depends on a model’s current quirks as disposable.
  4. 04 Assume near-universal use, not compliance. 94% of undergraduates use generative AI for assessed work (Higher Education Policy Institute, 2026).
  5. 05 Never require a paid tool. Study paid capability as an object, not an instrument.
  6. 06 Refusal is a legitimate, credited outcome. Choosing not to use AI, with reasons, earns credit too (Bali, 2023; Selwyn, 2019).
  7. 07 Keep the student’s own text in the room. Their draft, their prompt, their process log.
  8. 08 Make the developmental trade explicit. Show what offloading costs in learning, and let the student make the choice.
  9. 09 Don’t make it about integrity. Refer to policy; don’t teach from it.
  10. 10 Write for lifting. Modular enough for departmental colleagues to reuse without us.

What to stop doing

Reallocation needs subtraction. Offered as provocations, not instructions.

  • Standalone paraphrase-as-transformation teaching
  • Referencing format instruction — teach what referencing does instead
  • Generic "critical thinking" sessions
  • Sentence-level error correction as the main tutorial activity
  • Structure templates
  • Any provision premised on students not using AI

Use it tomorrow

One session, three texts. The stages that give the most value.

  1. 1Put a published text, a student text and a generated text side by side: same topic, same genre. Keep the prompt that produced the generated one.
  2. 2Map stance and hedging. Generated text hedges at about the right rate, in the wrong places (Hyland, 2005).
  3. 3Classify what each citation does. Generated citation mostly attributes and supports; it rarely contrasts or identifies a gap.
  4. 4Audit specificity: how many claims name who, what, when and how much? The most productive stage of all.
  5. 5Have students rewrite one generated paragraph until the discipline would accept it.

Where it falls short

  • Models may improve. If they gain real rhetorical positioning, the diagnostic activities lose value. The identity, process and institutional dimensions would survive. Claims about model behaviour should be re-tested every year.
  • The equity argument may cut both ways. Standardised prose may level a gatekeeping mechanism. Guard against romanticising difficulty (Warschauer et al., 2023).
  • The field may be claiming too much. It is convenient that the problem needs exactly this expertise. Discipline staff may hold the substance; this field may hold only the method.
  • The institution may not want it. Nobody may commission or read the analysis (Hadley, 2015). The answer is to do it inside teaching the institution already funds.
