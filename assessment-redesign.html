Assessment redesign map

Eight stages from the assessment you have to assessment that shows real learning, whether or not students use AI.

The question

How do I redesign a task so it really shows learning?

AI hasn't mainly created a cheating problem. It has exposed a validity problem: many tasks no longer show what we claim they show (Dawson et al., 2024). The map is a way to fix that by design, not by detection.

The map

The core test

Stage 3, for every outcome. Two questions, in this order.

  1. 1. Can AI do this to the standard we certify? Don't guess. Give the task to two or three current models, including the free one your students use, and mark the output against your rubric.
  2. 2. Is the effort the learning, or on the way to it? If the struggle is the learning, it's constitutive. If it's overhead, like formatting references or boilerplate code, it's preceding.
What to do, by whether AI can do the task and whether the effort is the learning
AI can't do it well AI can do it well
Effort is the learning Low risk. Keep it, and recheck each year. Redesign zone. Secure it, restructure it, or assess the process and judgement instead.
Effort comes before the learning Low value. Consider removing the task. Opportunity zone. Permit and teach AI use, and raise the level of the task.

Use it tomorrow

One task, limited time. Work down the list.

  1. 1Write, in one sentence, what this task lets you conclude about a student.
  2. 2Give the task to a current model and mark the output. Note the score.
  3. 3If it passes, decide: is the effort the learning, or on the way to it?
  4. 4If the effort is the learning, check whether the programme secures this outcome elsewhere.Yes: keep the task open, redesign it for learning, and add a process or dialogue element.No: make this task an assurance point, or create one elsewhere.
  5. 5If the effort comes before the learning, permit AI explicitly, teach its use, and raise what's assessed.
  6. 6Rewrite the criteria so they reward what AI can't supply.
  7. 7Tell students the AI stance for this task, and why.

Don't start here

  • With a detection policy. Detection tools are unreliable (Weber-Wulff et al., 2023) and biased against non-native English writers (Liang et al., 2023).
  • By turning coursework into exams wholesale. It swaps one validity problem for another, and harms the students inclusive assessment was designed to protect.
  • By writing a policy document. Without structural change to assessment, policy just moves the problem onto students and markers (Corbin, Dawson and Liu, 2025).

Where it falls short

  • Its leverage is at programme level. One module can run stages 2–8, but it can't fix outcomes the programme never secures.
  • The audit dates quickly. What AI can do changes each year, so the first question has to be asked again.
  • Oral assessment is only as reliable as its markers. Budget for rubrics, training and calibration.
  • Process evidence has costs. It can be faked, and asking it of everyone burdens students already managing disability, caring or paid work.
  • It needs institutional room to move. Permission to change mid-cycle, briefed external examiners, and a way to cut assessment volume without full revalidation.
  • The two-lane split is contested. Curtis (2025) argues that an all-or-none approach to AI is insupportable. Real practice sits on a gradient, which is why stage 8 sets the AI stance task by task.
