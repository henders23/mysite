AI terms for educators

Please note. These are suggestions and directions for exploration, not settled answers. They draw on published research and practice but have not been peer reviewed. Adapt what you use to your context, and check your institution’s policies before acting on it.

How to use this glossary

The terms run alphabetically in six groups. Technical terms are defined without technical language, and sources are given only where a term comes from a specific framework or study. The field moves quickly, so check the reviewed date.

A

  • Academic literacies

    An approach that treats academic reading and writing as social practices shaped by discipline, identity and power, rather than as generic skills to be fixed (Lea and Street, 1998).

    Why it matters in teaching: it explains why a one-off ‘how to prompt’ workshop is not enough. Good AI use depends on the genre and the discipline. See Academic literacies and AI on this site.

  • Agent (agentic AI)

    An AI system that does more than answer: it plans steps and takes actions, such as searching the web, running code, filling in forms or operating other software, with little human checking between steps.

    Why it matters in teaching: agents that work in a web browser can complete many online tasks from start to finish, including some quizzes and VLE activities. An unsupervised online task now tells you even less about who did the work.

  • AI Assessment Scale (AIAS)

    A five-level scale for telling students how far AI may be used in a task: No AI, AI Planning, AI Collaboration, Full AI and AI Exploration (Perkins, Roe and Furze, 2025). There is an adapted version for English for academic purposes (Roe, Perkins and Tregubova, 2026).

    Why it matters in teaching: it gives staff and students a shared language for assignment briefs. But a level printed on a brief does not control what students actually do. Only secured conditions can make ‘No AI’ hold.

  • AI detector

    Software that estimates how likely it is that a text was written by AI. It produces a score, not proof, and its accuracy varies with the tool, the type of text and the writer. In a 2023 study, detectors wrongly flagged essays by non-native English writers as AI-written far more often than essays by native writers (Liang et al., 2023).

    Why it matters in teaching: the OIA expects a provider to explain why it concluded that AI was used inappropriately, and to give the student a fair chance to respond (OIA, 2025). A detector score cannot carry that weight on its own.

  • AI literacy

    The set of abilities people need to evaluate AI critically, to communicate and work with it, and to use it as a tool (Long and Magerko, 2020).

    Why it matters in teaching: in practice it is specific to each discipline. Checking a legal citation and checking a chemical synthesis are different skills. See the 100 AI literacy activities on this site.

  • AI use declaration

    A statement in which students record whether and how they used AI in a piece of work.

    Why it matters in teaching: declarations can open useful conversations, but students often fail to declare AI use they have made (Gonsalves, 2025). Treat them as part of learning, not as proof of integrity.

  • Algorithmic bias

    A systematic skew in an AI system’s outputs that treats some people or groups unfairly. It usually comes from patterns in the training data or from design choices.

    Why it matters in teaching: it can show up in images that fall back on stereotypes, in feedback that treats one variety of English as the norm, and in detectors. Teach students to look for it, not just to work around it.

C–D

  • Cognitive offloading

    Using something outside your own head, such as a note, a calculator or now a chatbot, to reduce the mental effort a task needs (Risko and Gilbert, 2016).

    Why it matters in teaching: offloading is often sensible. The design question is whether it removes the very thinking the task exists to develop.

  • Context window

    The amount of text, measured in tokens, that a model can take into account at once. It includes your instructions, the conversation so far and any documents you upload.

    Why it matters in teaching: in long conversations or with large documents, earlier material may be dropped or used less reliably. Repeat key instructions, and check that the whole source was actually used.

  • Custom assistant

    A chatbot set up with its own instructions, and often its own documents, for a particular job, such as a revision tutor for one module. Many platforms let staff build these without code.

    Why it matters in teaching: it can keep AI on task for your module. But users can sometimes extract its instructions and files, so put nothing confidential in them.

  • Data protection impact assessment (DPIA)

    A documented assessment of the risks that a use of personal data poses to people, and of how those risks will be reduced. UK GDPR requires one when processing is likely to be high risk, and the ICO’s view is that the vast majority of AI uses are (ICO, 2023).

    Why it matters in teaching: if you plan to use AI on student work or student data, raise it with your data protection officer early.

  • Deepfake

    Realistic audio, images or video, made with AI, showing a real person saying or doing something they did not.

    Why it matters in teaching: it affects oral and video assessment, identity checks, and the safety of students and staff. Treat recordings as evidence that can be faked, and know your institution’s route for reporting image-based abuse.

E–H

  • Evaluative judgement

    The ability to make decisions about the quality of work, both your own and other people’s (Tai et al., 2018).

    Why it matters in teaching: when AI can produce fluent text in seconds, judging quality becomes a core learning outcome, and that includes judging AI output (Bearman et al., 2024).

  • Feedback literacy

    The understanding, abilities and dispositions students need to make sense of feedback and use it to improve their work or the way they learn (Carless and Boud, 2018).

    Why it matters in teaching: AI can supply feedback in abundance, but students still have to judge it and act on it. This idea underpins the Threshold Model on this site.

  • Fine-tuning

    Further training of an existing model on a smaller, specific set of examples, so that it behaves in a particular way or handles a particular field better.

    Why it matters in teaching: a supplier’s claim that a tool is ‘trained on your curriculum’ might mean fine-tuning, a search over your documents (see retrieval-augmented generation), or just a long set of instructions. Ask which.

  • Generative AI

    AI that produces new content, such as text, images, audio, video or code, in response to a request.

    Why it matters in teaching: it is a family of tools, not one product, and much of it is now built into everyday software such as word processors and search engines. ‘Did you use AI?’ is rarely a yes-or-no question.

  • Grounding

    Tying a model’s answer to specific sources, such as documents you supply or web search results, so that it can point to where its claims come from.

    Why it matters in teaching: grounding reduces made-up answers but does not remove them. A model can still misread or misquote a source, so students should check each citation against the source itself.

  • Hallucination

    A confident output that is false or invented, such as a reference that does not exist, a wrong date or a made-up quotation. Some people prefer the word ‘confabulation’.

    Why it matters in teaching: it follows from how these systems work, so it can be reduced but not ruled out. Checking sources is a core skill to teach, not an optional extra.

  • Human in the loop

    A design in which a person reviews, approves or can override what an AI system does before it has consequences.

    Why it matters in teaching: in assessment, the loop has to involve real judgement, not a quick click of approval. The Threshold Model on this site keeps every act of academic judgement human.

I–O

  • Institutionally licensed tool

    An AI tool your institution has a contract for. The contract usually sets terms on where data is stored, how long it is kept and whether it is used for training, and there is often a DPIA in place.

    Why it matters in teaching: it is the safe default for any work that involves students or their data. Personal accounts carry none of these protections. See Choosing AI tools and protecting data on this site.

  • Jailbreak

    A prompt designed to make a model ignore its safety rules or its instructions.

    Why it matters in teaching: rules built into a tool are not a security barrier. Assume that a determined user can get round them.

  • Knowledge cutoff

    The date after which a model has no information from its training. Tools with web search can look up newer material, but the underlying model does not know it.

    Why it matters in teaching: answers about recent policy, law or research may be out of date unless the tool cites a current source.

  • Large language model (LLM)

    A system trained on a very large amount of text to predict the next small piece of text (a token), then trained further, often using human ratings, to follow instructions and hold a conversation. Chatbots are built on LLMs.

    Why it matters in teaching: it produces likely-sounding text, not checked facts. That explains both its fluency and its errors.

  • Model card

    A short document from a model’s developer describing what the model is for, how it was tested, its known limits and the uses it is not suited to. The idea was proposed by Mitchell et al. (2019).

    Why it matters in teaching: it is the quickest place to check a tool’s stated limits before you adopt it.

  • Multimodal

    Able to take in or produce more than one kind of content, such as text, images, audio and video.

    Why it matters in teaching: students can photograph a problem sheet or hold a spoken conversation with a tool. A task cannot rely on its format (a diagram, a recording, a handwritten page) to keep AI out.

  • Open-weight model

    A model whose trained internal settings (its weights) are published, so that anyone can download it and run it on their own computers. This is not the same as fully open source, because the training data is often not released.

    Why it matters in teaching: running a model on institutional hardware can keep data inside the institution, but the institution then takes on responsibility for security and updates.

P–R

  • Personal data

    Any information relating to an identified or identifiable living person, including information that identifies them only when it is combined with other details.

    Why it matters in teaching: a student’s essay, feedback or grade is personal data, even without a name on it, if it can be linked back to them.

  • Prompt

    The instruction or input you give an AI tool, including any text, files or images you attach.

    Why it matters in teaching: a clear prompt helps, but no prompt turns an unchecked answer into a reliable one.

  • Prompt injection

    Instructions hidden in content that a model reads, such as a web page, a document or an email, which take over what the model does.

    Why it matters in teaching: agents that read the web can be misdirected this way, and hidden text in a submission can try to steer AI-assisted marking. Some staff hide instructions in assignment briefs to catch AI use. This misleads students who use screen readers, and it is easy to get round.

  • Pseudonymisation and anonymisation

    Pseudonymised data has its identifiers replaced, for example names swapped for codes, but whoever holds the key can still link it back to the person, so in their hands it is still personal data (ICO, no date). Anonymised data can no longer identify anyone by any means reasonably likely to be used.

    Why it matters in teaching: removing the name from a student’s essay is, at best, pseudonymisation.

  • Reasoning model

    A model that writes out a series of intermediate steps before it gives an answer. This tends to improve results on multi-step problems such as maths, coding and logic.

    Why it matters in teaching: the visible ‘reasoning’ is itself output to be checked. It is not a guaranteed account of how the answer was reached.

  • Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

    A technique in which a system first searches a set of documents for relevant passages, then passes them to the model to answer from (Lewis et al., 2020). Many ‘chat with your course materials’ tools work this way.

    Why it matters in teaching: answers are only as good as the documents and the search, and the model can still misuse what it finds.

S–W

  • Secured assessment

    Assessment under conditions that confirm who did the work and what help they had, such as invigilated exams, vivas, interactive orals and observed practice.

    Why it matters in teaching: rules and declarations alone do not secure a task; changing how the task is carried out does (Corbin, Dawson and Liu, 2025). The assessment redesign map on this site calls these secured points ‘assurance points’ and keeps them few.

  • Special category data

    Types of personal data that UK GDPR protects more strictly: data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs or trade union membership, and genetic data, biometric data used for identification, and data about health, sex life or sexual orientation (ICO, 2024).

    Why it matters in teaching: it turns up in adjustments, extenuating circumstances claims and reflective writing. Keep it out of AI tools unless your data protection officer has approved the use.

  • Sycophancy

    A model’s tendency to tell people what they seem to want to hear, by agreeing with their views or praising weak work. Research links it partly to training on human preference ratings (Sharma et al., 2024).

    Why it matters in teaching: a student who asks ‘is this good?’ may get flattery. Teach students to ask for the strongest objections instead.

  • System prompt

    Instructions, set by a tool’s provider or builder, that the model receives before your message. They shape its tone, rules and limits, and users usually cannot see them.

    Why it matters in teaching: two tools built on the same model can behave very differently, and a custom assistant’s behaviour depends heavily on its system prompt.

  • Temperature

    A setting that controls how much randomness goes into a model’s choice of words: low gives more predictable output, high gives more varied output. Most chat tools hide it.

    Why it matters in teaching: the same prompt can give different answers on different runs, so any single output is one sample, not the answer.

  • Token

    The unit of text a model reads and writes, often a short word or part of a longer one. Usage limits, prices and context windows are all measured in tokens.

    Why it matters in teaching: it helps explain why models can stumble on tasks that look trivial, such as counting the letters in a word.

  • Training data

    The text, images and other material a model learned from. Some providers also use people’s conversations to improve later models, unless the user opts out or an agreement rules it out.

    Why it matters in teaching: what goes into a consumer tool may not stay private, and the make-up of the training data shapes a model’s biases and blind spots.

  • Two-lane assessment

    An approach from the University of Sydney that divides assessment into two lanes: secured ‘lane 1’ tasks that assure learning, where AI use is controlled, and open ‘lane 2’ tasks where AI use is scaffolded and supported (Bridgeman, Liu and Weeks, 2024).

    Why it matters in teaching: it is widely discussed in UK programme design, but it is an institutional practice framework rather than a tested model, and critics argue that an all-or-none split misses the real range of AI use (Curtis, 2025).

  • Watermarking

    Embedding a hidden signal in AI-generated content so that it can later be identified as machine-made. Some providers watermark images, audio or text, but text watermarks can be weakened by paraphrasing or translation, and there is no universal checker.

    Why it matters in teaching: a watermark cannot tell you whether a student used AI, and the absence of one proves nothing.
