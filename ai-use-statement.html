Writing an AI-use statement for an assessment

How to tell students, task by task, what AI use is allowed, why, and how to declare it. Four templates you can adapt for your next brief.

For
Module leads and programme teams
You’ll get
An AI stance for each task, mapped to a shared scale, and four templates
Time
10-minute read
Last reviewed
November 2026

Please note. These are suggestions and directions for exploration, not settled answers. They draw on published research and practice but have not been peer reviewed. Adapt what you use to your context, and check your institution’s policies before acting on it.

What the statement is for

Students need to know, for each task, what AI use is acceptable and what would count as misconduct. UK complaints casework calls this clarity essential, and notes that institution-wide policy often needs module-level guidance alongside it (OIA, 2025). The assessment brief is where that guidance belongs.

A good statement does three jobs. It says what students may do, why, in terms of what the task is meant to show, and how to declare what they did. The reason matters most. A rule without one reads as arbitrary, and students work around it.

Don’t expect the declaration to carry the weight. At one UK business school, 74% of students did not declare their AI use despite a mandatory coversheet; fear of penalties, unclear guidance and uneven enforcement were among the reasons (Gonsalves, 2025). Treat the declaration as part of the work, taught and read, not a confession you collect.

Two lanes, one scale

Start with the lane, not the level. In the University of Sydney’s two-lane approach, lane 1 tasks are secured: in person, supervised, and placed sparingly across a programme to assure attainment. AI may or may not be allowed there, at the examiner’s discretion. Lane 2 tasks are open: AI use is not restricted, and where relevant it is scaffolded and taught (Bridgeman, Liu and Weeks, 2024). On the assessment redesign map these are the assurance points and the open tasks.

Then set the level. The AI Assessment Scale (AIAS) gives staff and students a shared vocabulary. The original scale (Perkins et al., 2024) was revised in 2025, with new level names and a new fifth level, AI Exploration (Perkins, Roe and Furze, 2025). The authors’ site now shows version 2.1: the same five levels, each with one clear statement (AI Assessment Scale, 2026). If your institution still uses the 2024 labels, such as ‘AI-assisted editing’, move the whole programme to the new ones. Don’t mix the two.

The revised scale is not a ladder of permission, and its authors are blunt that it is not a security tool: ‘if all you have is a colourful piece of paper and a hopeful expectation, you don’t have assessment security’ (Furze, 2025). Any level can run under supervision; only Level 1 requires it (AI Assessment Scale, 2026). In an open task, a level tells students which uses will help them learn and what you will assess. It cannot stop other uses, because you can’t see or prevent use in unsupervised work (Liu, 2024).

The two-lane split is contested. Curtis (2025) argues that an all-or-none approach is insupportable. The scale is how you work on that gradient, task by task. What it can’t do is make an open task secure.

Permission is not security

A permission you can’t enforce isn’t security. If a task must show what a student can do unaided, secure it with supervision or dialogue: an exam, supervised in-class work, an interactive oral, or a short viva on the work. If you can’t secure it, assume any use and write the criteria for that.

Write the statement in six moves

  1. 01Name what the task showsOne sentence: what can you conclude about a student from this task? It is where the assessment redesign map starts, and it becomes the reason you give students.
  2. 02Choose the laneSecured if the task is an assurance point; open if not. A take-home task is not secured because its brief says ‘no AI’.
  3. 03Set the level and give the reasonPick the AIAS level and explain it in terms of learning, for example: ‘Use AI to test your plan against objections. The marks go to the argument you defend in the viva.’ Set the default by level of study, because novices can’t yet spot a model’s errors. EAP teams can use the EAP-AIAS, which adapts the levels for language learning (Roe, Perkins and Tregubova, 2026).
  4. 04Say what will be assessedName the criteria that reward what AI can’t supply: judgement, specificity, decisions and the reasons for them. If a competent model with a good prompt would meet a criterion, make it a threshold or remove it.
  5. 05Say how to declareAsk what AI contributed, by type, with an example, not a yes/no tick. Use the declaration in a formative task first, and read what students write.
  6. 06Say how the work is verifiedFor secured tasks, state the conditions and the dialogic check. For open tasks, say whether a short conversation about the work may follow, and that it is routine, not an accusation. Name alternative formats in advance. Following University of Bristol v Abrahart [2024] EWHC 299 (KB), the EHRC advises that a method of assessment will rarely if ever be a competence standard, so an oral format is usually open to reasonable adjustment (EHRC, 2024). Check arrangements with your disability service.

(a) AI-use statement: open task

For a lane 2 task. Replace the bracketed text, keep the reasons, and cut anything that doesn’t apply.

AI use on this task: OPEN
AI Assessment Scale level: [3 AI Collaboration]

What this task shows: [One sentence, e.g. that you can build and defend an interpretation of X from primary evidence.]

Uses we recommend: You may use AI to [generate ideas / test your argument against objections / get feedback on drafts / improve clarity]. We recommend these because [reason tied to the learning outcome].

Uses that won’t help you: [e.g. letting AI choose your argument or write your analysis]. This task is open, so we will not police how you use AI. But the marks go to [your analysis of this module’s data / your decisions and your reasons for them], and fluent text without these will not pass.

What you are responsible for: Everything you submit. Check every claim, reference, quotation and number AI gives you. You may hand over control, but not responsibility.

How to declare: Complete the AI-use declaration at the end of your submission. [An honest declaration of the uses above will not lower your mark.]

How the work is verified: [e.g. Every student / A sample of students will have a 10-minute conversation about their work. This is routine.] If you need an alternative format, contact [name] by [date].

If you are unsure: Ask [named contact] before you submit.
This statement sits under [institution policy name and link].

(b) AI-use statement: secured task

For a lane 1 assurance point. The security comes from the conditions, so state them precisely.

AI use on this task: SECURED
AI Assessment Scale level: [1 No AI / or the level permitted under supervision]

What this task shows: [One sentence on the outcome this assurance point secures, e.g. that you can reason through an unseen case without support.]

Conditions: [Invigilated exam / supervised in-class writing / 15-minute interactive oral / viva on your project].
[No AI tools or personal devices are permitted.] OR [You may use [tool type] for [purpose] only, on [a university-provided device].]
Permitted materials: [list].

Why: This is one of [number] points in the programme where we need evidence of what you can do yourself. [Other tasks in this module are open.]

How it is verified: [Supervision by invigilators / The follow-up questions depend on your answers, so we can see your own reasoning.]

Adjustments: If you have a support plan or need an alternative format, such as [a written or one-to-one alternative to the oral], contact [name] by [date]. [Language accuracy is not assessed unless it is part of the outcome.]

Preparing: [You may use AI to prepare, e.g. for practice questions or mock orals. Practise without it too.]

Policy: [institution policy name and link].

Attach to every submission, open or secured. It uses the contribution taxonomy from the in-sessional EAP guide, so students say what AI did, not just whether they used it.

(d) Assignment brief: the AI section

A section to paste into any brief. Check the misconduct wording against your institution’s regulations before use.

USING AI IN THIS ASSIGNMENT

Lane: [Open / Secured]   AIAS level: [number and name]

In one line: [e.g. You may use AI to plan and to get feedback on drafts. The argument and analysis are what we mark, and you will discuss them in a 10-minute conversation.]

Why: [link to the learning outcome and what the task shows]

Uses that help: [2–4 uses]
Uses that won’t earn marks: [2–3 uses, each with its reason]

How it is marked: The criteria reward [judgement / use of this module’s cases or data / decisions and reasons]. [Fluency and structure are threshold requirements, not where marks are earned.] If you can’t explain your work in the conversation, it hasn’t met the outcome.

Your responsibilities: Verify every claim, reference, quotation and number. Keep your prompts, outputs and drafts until results are confirmed. Don’t put personal data, confidential material or others’ unpublished work into AI tools [not approved by the institution: list or link].

Declaring: Complete the AI-use declaration [attached / in the submission form].

Verification: [conversation / oral / in-class component / none]

Accessibility: [alternative formats and how to request them, by date]

Academic integrity: [Secured tasks: using tools not permitted above.] [All tasks: fabricated sources or data, or a false declaration.] These may be treated under [regulation name and link]. [Describe the local process, e.g. any concern is discussed with you first and you can respond.]

Questions: [contact] by [date]

Common mistakes

  • ‘No AI’ on a take-home task.It can’t be enforced, it penalises the honest, and detection won’t make it real. Secure the task or open it.
  • A level without a reason.Students follow rules they understand. Tie every permission to what the task shows.
  • A yes/no declaration.It invites a defensive ‘no’. Ask what AI contributed, by type, with an example.
  • Different words in every module.Agree one scale and one set of labels across the programme.
  • Criteria that still reward fluency.If AI meets a criterion, no statement can rescue it. Rebuild the criteria (stage 6 of the redesign map).
  • Treating a detector score as proof.UK casework expects detector output to be weighed with other evidence, shared with the student, and open to response (OIA, 2025).
  • An oral with no alternative.Dialogic verification needs adjustments planned in advance, not improvised (EHRC, 2024).
  • Writing it once.What AI can do changes every year. Review the statement whenever you review the task.
