Who it's for

People who have to make decisions about assessment, and want the reasoning as well as the steps.

  • Programme leaders rewriting an assessment map
  • Module convenors redesigning a task before the next validation deadline
  • Academic developers running workshops
  • EAP and academic literacies practitioners supporting students across disciplines
  • Quality teams working out what a degree classification now warrants

Twelve propositions

The argument of the guide in brief, from its executive summary.

  1. 01Treating AI as a cheating problem produces the wrong remedies: surveillance and detection instead of design, evidence and judgement.
  2. 02Assessment has two obligations, assurance and development, which often pull in different directions. Do not assume one task meets both.
  3. 03Make assurance sparse, secured, individually attributable and programme-level. This is a well-reasoned proposition with little outcome evidence outside medicine: pilot and evaluate it.
  4. 04Make other assessment open, authentic, appropriately tool-rich and designed for learning.
  5. 05Write into the regulations how assurance points govern credit and classification (gates, caps, pass/fail or weighting), or the classification rests on unsecured evidence (§4.10).
  6. 06Ask of every task: is the effort it demands constitutive of the learning, or does it merely precede it?
  7. 07Detection scores are never determinative. The provider must prove misconduct on the balance of probabilities, and the student must have a fair chance to respond (§4.8).
  8. 08Demote criteria that reward fluency, structure and coverage; reward selectivity, situated specificity, epistemic honesty and verification.
  9. 09A short conversation about a student’s own work is strong evidence, if it has an anticipatory alternative format, calibrated examiners and a costed plan. Orals are not automatically cheaper than essays.
  10. 10AI’s effect on learning depends on design: guardrailed tutoring can help; unrestricted answer-supply can harm. Scale formative AI feedback; keep summative judgement human.
  11. 11Redesign is disciplinary. Generic solutions reproduce the deficit model academic literacies research dismantled.
  12. 12A redesign that improves validity but widens an equity gap has failed. Track both.

What's inside

An executive summary, then five parts. Each section stands alone, so it can be lifted into a workshop or committee paper.

  1. Part 1What has actually changedWhy generative AI has exposed a validity problem rather than created a cheating one, and six shifts in what assessment now is.
  2. Part 2Theoretical foundationsEleven foundations from assessment scholarship, the learning sciences and academic literacies research, each with its design implication.
  3. Part 3The sector frameworks, comparedTEQSA, the two-lane approach and the AI Assessment Scale, with their critiques; the UK regulatory and legal frame (OfS, the OIA, the Equality Act and Abrahart, consumer law); and a three-layer model that brings them together.
  4. Part 4The practical mapThe eight-stage protocol with a feasibility gate (Stage 4b), a one-page decision sequence, twelve design moves, a criteria bank, inclusive design for oral assessment, a costed workload model, and how assurance points govern credit and classification.
  5. Part 5The example bankWorked redesigns across 26 disciplines with professional-body checks, two whole-programme maps, eleven anti-patterns, evaluation methods and templates.

A note on the evidence

References were checked in November 2026: journal articles published since 2020 against Crossref or publisher records, and legal, regulatory and web sources against the official page. The costs in the workload model are illustrative, built on stated assumptions. Read these sources with caution:

  • Kosmyna et al. (2025) is a preprint with a small sample (54 participants, 18 completing the final session) and has been methodologically challenged (Stanković et al., 2026). Suggestive, not settled.
  • Gerlich (2025) and Lee et al. (2025) are correlational and rely on self-report. Neither shows that AI use causes reduced critical thinking.
  • Jabarian and Imas (2025) is a working paper, not yet peer reviewed, on particular detectors at a particular time.
  • Luong and Lockhart (2025) is a company announcement; competition coordinators graded the answers but did not evaluate the system.
  • The Sydney two-lane documents are institutional practice papers, cited as the primary statement of the approach and read alongside the peer-reviewed critique by Curtis (2025).

The case for redesign does not rest on the cognitive-cost studies. It rests on validity, which was established well before generative AI arrived.
