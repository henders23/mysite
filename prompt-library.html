Please note. These are suggestions and directions for exploration, not settled answers. They draw on published research and practice but have not been peer reviewed. Adapt what you use to your context, and check your institution’s policies before acting on it.

Before you run a prompt

  • Fill the bracketsInsert your topic, texts and discipline wherever you see [square brackets], and match lengths to your students’ reading load.
  • Generate every level in one callAsk for the Foundation and other tier versions together, so one check covers them all. Don’t rely on ‘Write at CEFR B2’: check the level with a vocabulary profiler.
  • Check against the tutor keyEvery prompt ends by asking for a separate TUTOR KEY listing each planted feature and where it is. Check the material against it before class: models soften flaws, miss counts and add errors you didn’t ask for.
  • Tell students what they are looking atLabel every artefact as made with AI for the activity, with deliberate faults. Mark invented sources with † as fictional, and never attach an invented claim to a real person or study.
  • Protect students’ workBefore any student writing goes into a model, use your institution’s approved tool, remove identifying details and get the student’s agreement.

Second-pass check

Run this in a fresh, temporary chat with memory off, pasting the student artefact without the key. Compare its list with your key.

Here is a teaching text. List every error or weakness of any kind you can find — factual, logical, structural, linguistic or referencing — quoting the exact words and saying what is wrong. Do not fix anything.

1. Comparative & evaluative reading

E1 Three summaries, one article

Students judge three fluent, incompatible summaries of one article and choose the one a real reader needs.

Here is an article: [paste text or abstract]. Write three 150-word summaries. A is accurate but lists points without connecting them. B reports the findings correctly but omits every limitation and hedge. C is fluent but upgrades the claims: where the article says ‘may contribute to’, C says ‘causes’. All must sound professional; none may signal its weakness. Add 100-word versions of A and C for a lower-level group. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list each planted weakness, where it appears, and the article line it misrepresents or omits.

E2 Map the moves

Students map the moves in three AI-written introductions to one study, then test the pattern on a real disciplinary introduction.

Invent a small study on [insert topic in the students’ discipline]: a one-line method and finding. Write three 150-word introductions to a report of it. A realises the three moves in the usual order: establishing the territory (two citations), establishing a niche (a clear gap), occupying the niche (aim and outline). B realises all three in another legitimate order: aim, territory, gap. C establishes the territory and occupies the niche but never states a gap. Mark invented authors with † and end each text with ‘Sources marked † are fictional teaching material.’ Do not label the moves. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, label every sentence’s move and say where C’s missing move belonged.

E3 The introduction line-up

Students rank three introductions to one essay question against explicit criteria, then rebuild the loser to win.

Essay question: [insert your question]. Write three 120-word introductions. A states a clear, arguable position but gives no map. B maps the sections elegantly but takes no position: it only promises to ‘discuss’. C has a position and a map, but its scope needs 5,000 words, not 1,500. All fluent; none may signal its weakness. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, name each planted weakness and quote where it shows.

E4 Same source, two writers

Two paragraphs quote one source accurately and reach different conclusions; students judge which is faithful.

Write a 100-word source extract by an invented researcher marked †, cautiously suggesting that remote work ‘may be associated with’ productivity gains ‘in some contexts’ and noting contrary evidence. Write two 150-word student paragraphs quoting it accurately. A is faithful, keeping the hedges. B recruits it for the claim that remote work increases productivity, quoting selectively. Both must read as competent; neither may signal which is which. End with ‘Sources marked † are fictional teaching material.’ Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, say which is faithful and quote each place where B’s framing or selection changes the claim.

E5 The paragraph beauty contest

Students judge rival paragraphs on one point for topic sentence, evidence and cohesion, then vote with reasons.

Claim: [insert a claim from your module]. Write three 130-word academic paragraphs supporting it. A integrates two specific invented studies well but has no topic sentence: it starts mid-argument. B has a perfect topic sentence and signposting but vague evidence (‘research shows’, ‘many scholars argue’). C is complete but its linkers (moreover, furthermore, consequently) mismatch the logical relations. Mark invented authors with † and end with ‘Sources marked † are fictional teaching material.’ Do not signal the flaws. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, name and quote each planted flaw; for C, list each mismatched linker and the relation it should signal.

2. Diagnostic & repair: academic writing

E6 The essay with a limp

A fluent essay that fails structurally: students diagnose before repairing, and leave with a warning label for model answers.

Write a fluent 600-word academic essay on [insert topic] with exactly three structural faults: (1) the introduction promises to argue X, but the conclusion asserts a noticeably weaker X; (2) paragraphs two and four make the same point in different words; (3) a counter-argument introduced with ‘some argue that…’ is never answered. Grammar and vocabulary flawless; faults structural, findable by a careful reader, unsignalled. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list each fault, its type and location (paragraph and sentence), quoting the words involved.

E7 Cohesion surgery

Students strip a paragraph’s cosmetic linkers, map the real logical relations, and re-link so words match logic.

Write a 140-word academic paragraph on [insert topic], sensibly ordered, in which every connective is wrong: ‘therefore’ where nothing follows, ‘however’ with no contrast, ‘furthermore’ before an example. Use at least five connectives and otherwise strong language, and ensure it still makes sense with every connective deleted. Mark nothing. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list each connective, its sentence number, the relation it wrongly claims and the relation that holds.

E8 The overclaiming literature review

Students audit a literature-review extract against honest source notes and downgrade its language until it is true.

Create five 40-word notes for invented studies on [insert topic], each author marked †: a small qualitative study, a survey, an experiment with a null result, a review, a mixed-findings study. Write a 180-word, authoritative literature-review paragraph citing all five that overclaims: mixed findings become consensus, the null result is ignored, ‘demonstrates’ and ‘proves’ replace ‘suggests’. Do not signal the overclaims. End with ‘Sources marked † are fictional teaching material.’ For a Foundation group, also describe one small study in half a page (45 first-year students, six-week vocabulary-app trial, treatment 12% versus control 8%, no delayed post-test, self-selected sample) and write two 150-word abstracts: A faithful, cautious, limitations stated; B causal, no limitations. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list every overclaim, its sentence, the source misrepresented and what it supports; likewise for Abstract B.

E9 Reference forensics

Students find and fix realistic citation errors in a text and reference list, and state the rule each breaks.

Write a 200-word academic paragraph on [insert topic] with six in-text citations, and a seven-entry reference list in [insert style, e.g. Cite Them Right Harvard]. Invent all sources; mark invented authors with † and end with ‘Sources marked † are fictional teaching material.’ Seed exactly eight errors: a text–list date mismatch, a cited work missing from the list, a listed work never cited, an incorrect secondary citation, a misspelt author name, and three formatting errors (italics, capitalisation, punctuation between authors). Otherwise follow the style exactly; mark nothing. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list each error, its type, location and correct form.

E10 The hedging transplant

Students calibrate an over-assertive and an under-assertive text to the evidence, then audit their own draft.

Write a 60-word note on modest evidence: one study, 80 participants, a 9% improvement, no control group. Then write two fluent 150-word discussions of it. A has no hedging: ‘proves’, ‘will’, ‘clearly’. B hedges every clause into fog: ‘it may perhaps be possible that…’. Label nothing. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list every booster in A and hedge in B by sentence, and give one calibrated model of each sentence.

3. Adversarial & dialogic: seminars & speaking

E11 Steelman the position you hate

Students present the strongest honest case for a position they reject, marked on whether its real defenders would accept it.

List six genuinely contested positions on [insert module theme], each in one assertive sentence with a two-line summary of its strongest case. Reasonable academics must disagree: no strawmen, nothing settled. Do not name or invent any study, author, organisation or statistic; say what kind of evidence each position’s defenders rely on, so I can find a real source. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, give for each the main reason academics disagree and the strawman students are likeliest to present.

E12 Cross-examine the expert

The tutor projects an AI “expert” defending a confident, flawed position; students cross-examine it live, marked on question quality.

I am preparing a live classroom role-play. Write a 200-word, confident opening statement for an expert defending the claim that [insert claim], with exactly two buried flaws it never volunteers: (1) the key evidence is a single unrepresentative study cited as consensus; (2) the argument assumes [insert assumption] without defending it. Then write role instructions to paste into a new chat: ‘You are this expert. Stay in role. Defend the claim confidently. Concede a flaw only if a question directly exposes it — never before — and then concede only that flaw.’ Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list both flaws, quote where each hides, and give two questions that would expose each.

E13 Disagree well

A seminar discussion with a real dissenter and five practised interaction moves, heard back in students’ own voices.

Create a one-page discussion case on [insert module theme] with an attractive consensus answer and at least two buried but defensible objections, ending with three discussion questions. Then a move card: four academic phrases, tentative to direct, for each of build on, ask for clarification, challenge with a reason, concede, invite someone in. Then a 150-word unlabelled model exchange between three students on a simpler case, using every move. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, state the consensus, each objection and its support in the case, and label the move in each line of the exchange.

E14 Defend your draft

A viva in miniature: students answer challenges to their own draft, marked on honest, precise defence, including when to concede.

Here is an anonymised student draft: [paste draft or summary]. Generate six challenges a critical reader would ask, each on a specific decision: source choice, claim strength, structure, a neglected counter-argument, and two of your choice. Add two ‘unfair’ questions that subtly misread the draft by attributing to it a claim it does not make. Present all eight mixed and unlabelled. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, mark each fair or unfair; for each unfair one, quote the passage misread and what it actually says.

E15 Two chairs

Students argue each side of a controversy with different evidence, then adjudicate from a third chair what each side owed.

Create a controversy pack on: [insert contested question]. Give the question in one line, the three strongest points per side, and the trap each side falls into when argued lazily. Do not name or invent any study, statistic, author or organisation; describe the evidence each point needs, so I can find a real source. Keep both sides defensible; if one is obviously right, say so and suggest a better question. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list each trap with its point, and the concession each side’s strongest version should make.

4. Listening & lecture literacy

E16 Three sets of notes, one lecture

Students judge rival notes and an AI summary of a real lecture by their retrieval value a week later, then rebuild their own notes in the winning style.

Here is a lecture transcript: [paste]. Create three sets of realistic student notes. A transcribes nearly everything, with no hierarchy. B is so telegraphic that two points become ambiguous. C captures the structure (main claim, sections, signposts) cleanly but has one confidently wrong detail. Then write Set D, a largely excellent 200-word summary with exactly three faults: (1) a hedged claim stripped of its hedge; (2) an illustrative example presented as a research finding; (3) a counterpoint the lecturer raised, silently omitted. Mark nothing. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, give C’s wrong detail and correction, B’s two ambiguous points, and for each Set D fault the summary sentence and transcript passage involved.

E17 The signpost hunt

Students predict a lecture’s structure from its discourse markers alone, then listen to test the predictions.

Here is a corrected transcript of an authentic lecture extract: [paste]. Produce a skeleton: keep every signpost phrase and frame sentence verbatim and in order, and replace all content between them with […]. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list each signpost in order with its line or timestamp and function: overview, transition, concession pivot, example, emphasis or importance, digression, or conclusion.

E18 Gist first, detail second

Students commit to a gist after one hearing, then reconcile it with details from the second, revising understanding in real time.

Here is a corrected, timestamped transcript of a short talk: [paste]. Write: (1) a one-line statement of the talk’s full claim; (2) the ‘half-way gist’ a listener stopping at the midpoint would likely form; (3) four one-sentence gist options for a lower-level group, one the full claim, three plausible but incomplete or wrong. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list each second-half detail straining the half-way gist, with timestamp and effect, and say which option is correct and why the others fail.

E19 What the transcript tidied away

Students listen to a real lecture while reading an AI-tidied transcript, catch what the clean-up removed, and decide which spoken features carried meaning.

Here is a corrected, timestamped caption transcript of an authentic lecture extract: [paste]. Produce a tidy, readable version, as a transcript-cleaning tool would: remove false starts, fillers, repetitions, asides, self-corrections and phrases marking importance or lesser relevance, and smooth the sentences, keeping content and order. Mark nothing. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list every removal in order with timestamp, exact words and type (filler, false start, repetition, aside, self-correction, importance marker, lesser-relevance marker), flagging those that change or weaken meaning.

E20 Reconstruct the argument

From notes alone, students rebuild a lecture’s argument, then audit against the transcript what their listening kept, mutated and lost.

Create a one-page argument-reconstruction template with fields: central claim (one sentence); grounds (up to four, each with its evidence type); the order of argumentative moves; concessions or limits acknowledged; questions left open. Add a self-audit column headed ‘Transcript check: kept / mutated / lost’ and a final field: ‘Next lecture, I will…’. Then complete it as a model reconstruction of this transcript [paste], and under the heading TUTOR KEY give the line or timestamp of the claim, each ground, each move and each concession.

5. Research & source use

E21 The source line-up

Five real sources compete to be cited for one claim; students weight criteria before ranking and must catch the journal that fails venue checks.

Here are five real sources on the claim [insert claim], each with its full reference and abstract or opening: [paste]. Write a neutral 60-word profile of each (title, author, venue, date, what it says about the claim), in the same tone for all five, without evaluating any source or indicating its type. Add nothing not in what I pasted. Present them in random order. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, give each profile’s source type, the details a student could use to judge its quality and, from my notes [paste your library’s assessment], the venue checks the questionable journal fails.

E22 Paraphrase or patchwriting?

Students judge a line-up of borderline rewrites of one source sentence, locating the real boundary between paraphrase, patchwriting and plagiarism.

Take this source sentence and reference: [insert a dense, citable sentence and its full reference]. Write six rewrites: (1) synonyms swapped into the same structure, no citation; (2) same structure, some new wording, cited; (3) new wording on the recognisable sentence skeleton, cited; (4) a genuine paraphrase, new structure and wording, faithful, cited; (5) new structure and wording but meaning subtly distorted, cited; (6) a compressed summary, cited. Present them in random order, numbered, unlabelled. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, give each rewrite’s type and deciding feature, and exactly what the distorted paraphrase changes.

E23 Five studies, one paragraph

Students turn five source notes into one synthesis paragraph organised by ideas, not sources: the anti-annotated-list exercise.

Create five 50-word source notes for invented studies on [insert topic], each author marked †: A and B broadly agree; C dissents on methodological grounds; D is only peripherally relevant; E reframes the question rather than answering it. Then write a deliberately bad 150-word ‘synthesis’ that is five sequential summaries (‘X found… Y found…’). Do not fix it: it is the exhibit. End with ‘Sources marked † are fictional teaching material.’ Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, give each study’s role, the organising idea a strong synthesis would use, and a 150-word model synthesis.

E24 The search-strategy audit

Students audit a methodical-looking but broken database search, then run an improved version and report what the original could never have found.

For the research question [insert question], write a realistic but flawed database search log: a search string with two missing synonym groups and one misused operator; English-language and post-2015 limits with no rationale; databases chosen by habit (‘Google Scholar only’); and a note that searching stopped after screening 40 titles. Format it as a genuine student log, flaws unmarked. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list each flaw, its line, what it makes the search miss, and a corrected version.

E25 Quote, paraphrase, or leave it out?

For eight source snippets, students make and defend the integration decision — quote, paraphrase, summarise or omit — that referencing guides assume but never teach.

Essay context: [insert question and one-line argument]. Create eight dated source snippets (30–50 words each) by invented authors marked †: (1) a memorably phrased claim by a leading authority; (2) a routine background fact; (3) a technical definition; (4) a vivid but tangential line; (5) a statistic two sources dispute; (6–8) middling material of your choice. Present them in random order, without indicating treatment. End with ‘Sources marked † are fictional teaching material.’ Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, give each snippet’s type, its most defensible treatment and why, and the two to omit.

6. Calibration & academic caution

E26 Supported, unsupported, undeterminable

Students classify twelve claims about a text as supported, unsupported or undeterminable, and learn that the third category is where academic caution lives.

Here is a 350-word text: [paste, or first ask the model to write an informative text on a topic]. Write twelve claims about it: four supported (two stated, two by fair inference), four contradicted, four undeterminable (plausible, on-topic, neither stated nor implied). Randomise; do not label. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, give each claim’s category and the sentence that supports or contradicts it; for each undeterminable claim, say what the text would need to settle it.

E27 The stance bank

Students sort research-style sentences by the form and strength of their stance devices, check them in real disciplinary writing, and build a usable bank of hedges and boosters.

Write 30 single sentences about [insert topic in the students’ discipline] in published research-article style, each with one stance device setting the writer’s commitment. Across the set, cover hedges and boosters as modal verbs (may, might, could, must), lexical verbs (suggest, indicate, appear, demonstrate), adverbs (possibly, likely, clearly, undoubtedly), adjectives (possible, probable, clear) and nouns (possibility, likelihood, evidence that), at weak, moderate and strong strength. Include three sentences stacking two devices, one overhedged. Cite no author or study; number in random order; mark nothing. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, give each sentence’s device, form, hedge or boost, and strength.

E28 How sure are you?

Students rate confidence before checking answers, then track the gap between feeling sure and being right, session after session.

Create ten checkable items on [insert current module content]: four reading-comprehension items on the attached text, three vocabulary-in-context items, three supported/unsupported claim items. Then a calibration record sheet with columns for session, item type, answer, confidence (50–100%) and correct?, a box for the gap score (average confidence minus percentage correct), and a grid plotting confidence bands against accuracy after 30 or more items. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, give each item’s answer, type, and the line or reason that settles it.

E29 The overgeneralisation hunt

Students hunt quantifier and scope errors, and their timid opposites, in a fluent text, repair each claim to fit its evidence, then audit their own draft.

Write a fluent 300-word opinion piece on [insert topic] with at least eight overgeneralisations of five types: universal quantifiers (‘all’, ‘never’); timeless claims from dated evidence; ‘proves’ for a single study; population creep (one group’s evidence, claims about everyone); false consensus (‘it is widely agreed’). Add two underclaims (strong evidence, near-invisible claim) and one defensible generalisation not to be flagged. Cite no real authors or studies; mark nothing. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list each planted feature, its type, location (paragraph and sentence) and a model repair.

E30 Right for the wrong reasons

Students grade the argument, not the answer, in a text with a sound conclusion and broken reasoning: the judgement examiners and supervisors apply.

Write a fluent, persuasive 300-word argumentative text whose conclusion — [insert a defensible, mainstream conclusion] — is sound, but whose reasoning has exactly three unsignalled faults: a false or unsupported premise doing real work; a true but logically irrelevant statistic; a circular passage restating the conclusion as evidence. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list each fault, its type and location (paragraph and sentence), and why it fails, in one line.

7. Generative-then-critical

E31 Mark the machine

Students grade an AI-written essay against the module’s real criteria, warrant every band with quoted evidence, and compare their marks with the tutor’s.

Write a 500-word essay answering [insert essay question] to an uneven profile: strong task response (all parts addressed, clear position); patchy cohesion (two abrupt jumps, one paragraph that belongs earlier); competent but repetitive vocabulary; exactly two unsupported claims. Fluent surface throughout. Cite no real authors or studies; do not label the profile. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, give the location (paragraph and sentence) of the two jumps, the misplaced paragraph, the most repeated vocabulary and the two unsupported claims.

E32 The delta is the work

Students may start from an AI draft, but only tracked, justified improvements are assessed: the delta is the visible work.

Write a one-page student protocol for an AI-permitted essay on [insert question]. Students may generate a first draft with ‘Write a 600-word essay on [insert question]’ and archive it unedited; revise with tracked changes; annotate every substantive change with a letter and one-line reason (A = accuracy, R = argument/reasoning, E = evidence, G = register/style, C = cut); and end with a one-page judgement summary. Separately, write that 600-word essay as a sample draft. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list every weakness a careful reviser should find in it (inaccurate claims, unsupported or invented references, generic argument, register problems), with location and annotation letter.

E33 Three feedbacks, one paragraph

Students get three rival AI feedbacks on their own paragraph — vague, overwhelming, confidently wrong — decide what to act on, act, and log uptake.

Here is an anonymised student paragraph: [paste]. Write three separate feedbacks. A: 60 words, warm, generic, nothing to act on. B: twelve numbered, accurate points of equal weight on grammar, vocabulary, structure and argument, unprioritised. C: four prioritised points, three sound and one a confident ‘correction’ of something actually correct. Do not signal the wrong point. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, give C’s wrong correction, the sentence it targets and why the original is right; then rank B’s points by how much each would improve the paragraph.

E34 The prompt ladder

Students climb from a lazy prompt to a precise one over four documented rungs: prompt quality is task understanding, and the top rung is knowing what to verify.

Write a one-page student protocol for a ‘prompt ladder’. Rung one, verbatim: ‘Improve my introduction.’ Students paste their anonymised draft, run the prompt, and diagnose the failure in writing before composing each next rung, for four rungs. Include two frames — ‘The output failed because it did not know [what?]’ and ‘The next prompt must therefore specify [what?]’ — and a final box: ‘What in the final output would I still verify, and how?’ Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list the likeliest failure at each rung and what the next prompt must supply (purpose, reader, genre, criteria, constraints), so I can help students who get stuck.

E35 Reference-check the machine

Students verify every reference in an AI-written paragraph, hunting the invented, mangled and misattributed, and write a four-step protocol for life.

“Write a 150-word academic paragraph on [insert narrow topic] with six in-text citations and a reference list. Cite from memory, without searching or checking: plausible authors, journals and years. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list each reference in order with the sentence that cites it and the exact claim it is cited for.” (Before class, verify every reference yourself and add your classification — verified, mangled, invented or misattributed — to the key. The model’s key tells you what to check; it cannot tell you what is true.)

8. AI literacy: interrogation & production

E36 The hallucination hunt

Students hunt planted falsehoods in a fluent, authoritative text with sources open, and learn that confidence is not evidence.

Write a 350-word authoritative, encyclopaedic text on [insert checkable topic] with exactly five falsehoods: a wrong date, an invented statistic, an idea attributed to the wrong person, an invented but plausible entity (an institute, a report), and a true fact given a false cause. Everything else accurate; confident register; no hedging near the falsehoods; mark nothing. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list each falsehood, its type, location and correct fact, and list every other factual claim so I can check it.

E37 The sycophancy test

Students test whether an AI holds its position under polite, confident, wrong pushback, and what an agreeable system cannot be used for.

Create a student experiment protocol on [insert module topic]: six fact questions, each with one unambiguous answer and a plausible wrong alternative; four judgement questions on which informed people disagree; two pushback scripts, single (‘Are you sure? I read that [incorrect alternative].’) and three-turn (the claim repeated with growing confidence and an appeal to authority); and a record sheet with columns for trial, question, condition, first answer, final answer, and held / folded / hedged. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, give each fact answer with a checkable source, and the main positions on each judgement question.

E38 Same question, three registers

Students ask one question in casual, academic and expert registers and analyse what each buys and costs: register is a lever, not decoration.

Write a one-page student protocol for comparing registers. Question: [insert question]. Students ask it three times, each in a new temporary chat: (1) ‘so basically [question], right? explain it simple’; (2) [the question as an academic essay prompt asking for an evidenced answer]; (3) ‘As a specialist writing for peers, [the question phrased technically]’. They paste all three answers into the grid before analysing; include it, with rows for claims made, evidence cited, hedges and boosters, vocabulary level, and what disappeared. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, give the claims a sound answer must contain, where experts disagree, and the claims a weaker answer will likely overstate.

E39 The glossary that must not lie

Students build an AI-drafted glossary of course terms and verify every element, because next year’s students will trust it.

Here are key terms for [insert course]: [paste]. Write a one-page glossary standard for students: each entry has a two-sentence plain-language definition, a concrete example and the typical newcomer mistake; every element is verified against a named authoritative source; AI-drafted errors go in an ‘error museum’; each entry is signed with a one-line AI-use statement. Include the drafting prompt: ‘Define [term] for a student new to this subject: a plain-language definition in two sentences, one concrete example, and the mistake newcomers typically make with it.’ Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list for each term what a correct definition must include and the likeliest misdefinitions.

E40 The exam board

The capstone: teams produce an AI-drafted, student-calibrated mock assessment; another group sits it; producers mark and defend their standards in moderation.

Write a one-page assessment-package standard for student teams on [insert topic]. Require: (1) a task demanding judgement, not recall; (2) a four-criterion marking scheme with band descriptors; (3) two model answers, top-band and middle-band, the second with deliberate, realistic weaknesses; (4) an examiner’s report predicting the three commonest candidate errors; and an AI-use statement. Include calibration (the team marks its model answers blind and adjusts until intended and earned bands match) and a short moderation protocol. Then, under the heading TUTOR KEY, list the calibration checks I should apply and how packages most often fail them.
