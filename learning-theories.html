Learning theories that matter now

Six theories that help you decide what AI should and shouldn’t do in your teaching, each with what AI changes, what to do next week, and two readings to start with.

Please note. These are suggestions and directions for exploration, not settled answers. They draw on published research and practice but have not been peer reviewed. Adapt what you use to your context, and check your institution’s policies before acting on it.

Why theory, and why now

Most AI guidance is about tools and rules, and both date quickly. Learning theory lasts longer, because it describes what students have to do in order to learn, whatever technology is in the room.

AI puts pressure on each of the six theories below. Each item gives the idea, what AI changes, what to do in your teaching and two readings. Where a theory connects to one of my frameworks, the item says so.

Six theories

  • Evaluative judgement

    The idea. Evaluative judgement is the capability to make decisions about the quality of your own work and other people’s (Tai et al., 2018). It means knowing what good looks like in a field, and being able to apply that standard to a particular piece of work. Students build it through self-assessment, peer review, exemplars and dialogue about criteria.

    What AI changes. Producing competent work is now cheap; judging it is not. Bearman et al. (2024) argue that students need to judge the quality of AI outputs and of AI processes, and that AI can also be used to develop students’ own judgement. Judgement becomes the scarce, assessable capability.

    In your teaching. Give students two or three AI responses to the same task, ask them to rank the responses against your criteria, and have them state the standard they used. Calibrate against exemplars before peer review. The Threshold Model keeps every graded judgement human for the same reason.

    Key readings: Tai et al. (2018); Bearman et al. (2024).

  • Feedback literacy

    The idea. Feedback literacy is the understanding, capacities and dispositions students need to make sense of feedback and use it to improve their work or learning strategies (Carless and Boud, 2018). Carless and Boud name four features: appreciating feedback, making judgements, managing affect and taking action. Feedback only counts when the student does something with it.

    What AI changes. Feedback used to be scarce. Now it is unlimited and instant, so the bottleneck moves from supply to uptake. Students must judge which comments are credible and which matter, and act on them rather than accept rewritten text. A learning-centred view, in which students seek and use feedback rather than simply receive it, fits this situation well (Molloy, Boud and Henderson, 2020).

    In your teaching. Keep the kinds of feedback distinct, as the Threshold Model’s feedback ladder does: AI verdicts and questions for practice, peer deliberation, and human judgement at the point of grading. Before you accept the next draft, ask for a short written response to the feedback: what the student will change, and why.

    Key readings: Carless and Boud (2018); Molloy, Boud and Henderson (2020).

  • Academic literacies

    The idea. Lea and Street (1998) set out three models of student writing: study skills, academic socialisation and academic literacies. The third treats writing as social practice, bound up with disciplinary ways of knowing, identity and power, and finds students’ difficulties in gaps between their expectations and their tutors’ rather than in individual deficit. The models are nested, not alternatives.

    What AI changes. AI makes the surface of academic writing easy to produce, so fluency no longer proves understanding. Anson (2024) uses Lea and Street’s framework to examine what large language models mean for students’ literacy development. Institutional responses to AI tend to replay the three models: prompt workshops, induction into norms and, more rarely, critical engagement with AI use as a practice.

    In your teaching. Work at the academic literacies level. Put a published text, a student text and a generated text in your discipline side by side, and discuss authority, stance and voice. The academic literacies page sets out a full text-analysis protocol and seven dimensions for in-sessional provision.

    Key readings: Lea and Street (1998); Anson (2024).

  • The ICAP framework

    The idea. ICAP sorts students’ observable engagement into four modes: passive (receiving), active (manipulating material, such as highlighting or copying), constructive (generating something beyond the material, such as an explanation or a concept map) and interactive (co-constructing ideas with a partner, where both contribute) (Chi and Wylie, 2014). Learning is predicted to increase from passive to interactive. The framework classifies what students do, not what the activity is called.

    What AI changes. The same tool can sit at any level. A student who pastes in a question and copies the answer is passive or active; one who explains their reasoning and has it challenged may be constructive. When the AI generates the explanation, the constructive work has moved to the machine.

    In your teaching. Audit your AI activities by asking who is generating. Have students produce the explanation, prediction or argument first, and let the AI respond, not the reverse. Chi et al. (2018) report what happened when teachers translated ICAP into their own lesson plans, which is useful before you redesign a module.

    Key readings: Chi and Wylie (2014); Chi et al. (2018).

  • Desirable difficulties and cognitive offloading

    The idea. Some conditions that make learning feel harder and slow visible progress, such as spacing, interleaving, self-testing and varied practice, produce more durable learning (Bjork and Bjork, 2011). The key distinction is between performance, which you can see during learning, and learning, the lasting change you have to infer. Cognitive offloading is using physical action or external tools to reduce a task’s mental demands, from writing a list to setting a reminder; how much people offload depends partly on their beliefs about their own abilities, which can be inaccurate (Risko and Gilbert, 2016).

    What AI changes. AI is the most powerful offloading tool yet, and it removes difficulties without discrimination, the desirable along with the rest. Performance with AI can rise while unaided learning falls, which is the pattern in the strongest recent studies (see What the research says).

    In your teaching. Decide which difficulties are the learning, and protect them: retrieval, the first draft of an argument, working a problem through. The Threshold Model names two to four protected difficulties per module where AI is switched off. Let AI carry the load that isn’t the point, such as formatting.

    Key readings: Bjork and Bjork (2011); Risko and Gilbert (2016).

  • Legitimation Code Theory: semantic waves

    The idea. Legitimation Code Theory describes knowledge partly through two variables: semantic gravity, how far meaning depends on its context, and semantic density, how much meaning is condensed into a term or practice (Maton, 2013, 2014). Cumulative knowledge-building depends on semantic waves: moving from abstract, condensed ideas to concrete, context-bound examples and back again. Teaching that unpacks ideas without repacking them rides what Maton calls a down escalator.

    What AI changes. AI can unpack any concept on demand, in simpler words, with a worked example or an analogy, so the down escalator is always available. The harder move, repacking a case into the discipline’s own concepts, is the one students most need to practise and the easiest one to skip.

    In your teaching. Ask students to profile an AI explanation: where does it move up and down, and where does it stay flat? Then have them write the repacking step themselves, linking the case back to theory. The Wavelength app offers primers, worked examples and a semantic-wave studio for practising this.

    Key readings: Maton (2013); Maton (2014).

One thread runs through all six

Each theory locates learning in something the student must do: judge, act on feedback, take a position, generate, struggle, repack. Design AI use so that it supports that work rather than doing it.
