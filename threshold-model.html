The Threshold Model

Abundant feedback. Singular judgement. A model for learning, feedback and assessment in which AI multiplies practice and response, and every act of academic judgement stays human.

The question

Where should AI give feedback, and where must a human judge?

Feedback has always been rationed by staff time, and usually arrives after the work is finished, when it can no longer be used (Sadler, 1989; Boud and Molloy, 2013). AI removes the scarcity. The model uses that, without letting AI anywhere near the grade.

The core move: separate the economy of feedback from the economy of judgement. Put AI wholly inside the first, and wholly outside the second.

The rigour rule: the standard is common and non-negotiable; the route to it is individual and adaptive.

The model

Three zones, two crossings, three layers.

Three zones

  1. The Workshop Rehearsal. Frequent, low-stakes and ungraded by design. Failure is free and repetition unlimited. Each module protects two to four difficulties where AI is switched off. AI: instant developmental feedback, Socratic questioning and adaptive practice. The zone AI transforms most.
  2. The Commons Dialogue. Seminars, studios, moots and peer critique, where human talk is the primary technology. Peers calibrate against exemplars before reviewing each other (Nicol, 2021). AI: only as opponent, stakeholder or amplifier. Never answering what students could answer.
  3. The Bench Judgement. Human, accountable and standard-referenced, usually twice per module. Judged on the artefact, the student's account of it, and a live conversation. AI: none. Structurally excluded: no AI marks, feedback text, ranking, triage or detector output.

Two crossings

  • The ThresholdThe crossing into judgement. The student brings the artefact, three to five curated Workshop items with a rationale, and a specific AI-use declaration.
  • The ReturnWithin ten working days: the judgement plus three feedforward targets. It isn't complete until the student responds in writing (Carless and Boud, 2018).

Three layers

  • The SpinePersonalisation. A student-owned map of 12 to 20 programme capabilities, gathering evidence against each. Never used for progression or misconduct decisions.
  • ProvocationThe sparkle. Simulations, awkward clients, counterfactuals and adversaries, each debriefed by a person.
  • The WarrantIntegrity, with no detection component: declaration, a decision record kept during the work, a live defence, and one assessment per module where AI is genuinely unavailable.

The feedback ladder

Four kinds of feedback. Confusing them is the commonest failure in AI-augmented course design.

Ten principles

  1. 01 Judgement is a human act, and it is signed. A named person makes and can justify every judgement. AI may not generate, propose, rank, pre-mark, moderate or recommend a grade.
  2. 02 Abundance in feedback, scarcity in judgement. Raise formative response by an order of magnitude and cut summative points to the minimum: four graded assessments become two judgement points and thirty-plus feedback events.
  3. 03 Personalisation adapts the route, never the standard. Sequence, pace, practice, context and provocation vary. Criteria and level descriptors never do.
  4. 04 AI questions; humans answer for. AI’s default register is a question. Its instructions are a designed, versioned curriculum artefact written by academics.
  5. 05 Desirable difficulty is protected. Each module names its productive difficulties and designs AI out of them (Meyer and Land, 2003).
  6. 06 Talk is the primary technology. AI multiplies conversations but doesn’t replace the ones that matter, and contact hours are protected (Laurillard, 2002).
  7. 07 Process is evidence, and provenance is assessable. Students keep a decision record and declare AI use. Honest, extensive use isn’t penalised; misdeclaration is misconduct.
  8. 08 Every student is answerable, live, for their own work. A short, unscripted conversation at every judgement point: the single highest-value change in the model.
  9. 09 No detector may be used as evidence. Detectors are unreliable and biased against writers of English as an additional language. Suspicion is resolved by conversation (Liang et al., 2023).
  10. 10 Delight is designed, not incidental. The sparkle layer is a first-class design object with its own share of curriculum time.

Three disciplines

Judgement weighs the artefact, the student's account of it, and their answers. The balance follows the discipline.

  • Engineering
    Artefact 45%
    Account 25%
    Answering 30%
    Signature task. Find the buried defect in a plausible, AI-generated design package. Answering. A 15-minute design review with two academics and a live change of requirement. Distinctive rule. An unverified simulation result presented as fact counts as a professional error.
  • Law
    Artefact 35%
    Account 20%
    Answering 45%
    Signature task. Verify every authority in a fluent, partly fabricated memorandum. Answering. Oral advocacy or a client exercise, 12 to 20 minutes, with interventions from the bench, building on simulation-based legal education (Maharg, 2007). Distinctive rule. One fabricated or misdescribed authority caps the artefact mark. In Mata v. Avianca (2023), counsel filed a brief citing cases that did not exist.
  • EAP
    Artefact 30%
    Account 30%
    Answering 40%
    Signature task. AI rewrites one of the student’s paragraphs five ways; the student explains what each version does. Answering. A 10 to 15 minute tutorial defence of textual choices. Distinctive rule. The account weighs as much as the text, because the rationale is the learning outcome (Lea and Street, 1998).

A single institutional policy would be too permissive for law, too restrictive for EAP, and largely irrelevant to engineering.

Use it tomorrow

Start with one module.

  1. 1Cut the graded assessments to two judgement points, and move the rest into ungraded practice and feedback.
  2. 2Name the module's two to four protected difficulties, and design AI out of them.
  3. 3Write the AI tutor's instructions yourself: withhold the answer, one question at a time, hints only on evidence of effort.
  4. 4At each judgement point, ask for a specific AI-use declaration and hold a short, unscripted conversation of 8 to 12 minutes.
  5. 5Return each judgement within ten working days with three feedforward targets, and require a written response.

Where it falls short

  • It is not a workload saving. Close to neutral in year one, modestly positive after. Marking falls; oral time is the largest new cost and has to be timetabled.
  • It needs regulations behind it. Without an explicit ban on AI in judgement, workload pressure erodes the principle within two years.
  • Oral defence carries equity risk. Give written questions at the start, thinking time and an alternative format on request, and assess substance rather than fluency (Tai et al., 2023).
  • Scale changes the design. Above about 300 students: one conversation per student per year, plus targeted ones where the artefact and account don't match.
  • Access must be equal. The institution has to provide the practice environment, or the model creates two tiers of student.
