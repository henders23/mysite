Generative AI and Academic Literacies in In-sessional EAP

A critical and practical guide for in-sessional EAP practitioners, covering theory, frameworks, classroom activities, provision design and practitioner development.

Download the full guide (Word) The wider framework: academic literacies and AI

Please note. These are suggestions and directions for exploration, not settled answers. They draw on published research and practice but have not been peer reviewed. Adapt what you use to your context, and check your institution’s policies before acting on it.

Who it’s for

In-sessional EAP practitioners and academic skills staff who work alongside disciplinary programmes, and the centre leads and heads of department who plan their provision and staff development. It assumes you already know genre, move and stance analysis, and shows how to turn that toolkit on AI-generated and hybrid text.

Six key ideas

  • Prompting is the new study skillsGeneric “AI skills” modules repeat the old study-skills model. Good AI use depends on the discipline and genre, so AI teaching belongs inside discipline-embedded provision.
  • The three-orientations auditAny AI policy, workshop or task can be read as AI-as-skills, AI-as-socialisation or AI-as-literacies. The task is to move provision towards the third without dropping the first two.
  • EAP's toolkit is an AI-critical toolkitMove analysis, metadiscourse audits and stance analysis make AI output open to inspection. The aim is evaluative judgement, not spotting the machine.
  • Detection is an equity issueAutomated detectors are unreliable and can disadvantage multilingual writers. In-sessional provision is well placed to explain how they work, what policy says and how students can keep their writing process visible.
  • Disclosure as habit, not confessionA contribution taxonomy (ideation, structuring, language repair, register, content, source work, feedback) replaces a yes/no declaration and is practised in low-stakes formative work.
  • Adaptation from strengthIn-sessional practitioners do not need retraining from scratch. Their existing expertise, developed through practitioner inquiry, is what institutions need now.

What’s inside

  1. Part One: Theoretical foundationsReads the post-2022 AI literature through the academic literacies tradition (Lea and Street, Lillis and Scott, Ivanič) and condenses the result into five theses and a working definition of critical AI academic literacies.
  2. Part Two: FrameworksSix frameworks, each with its source, an EAP adaptation and a caveat: a genre-specified five-part framework for AI-assisted writing, a three-orientations audit, the AI Assessment Scale and its EAP version, critical AI literacy, a contribution taxonomy for disclosure, and discourse-analytic probes. A decision guide shows which framework answers which question.
  3. Part Three: Practice activitiesEleven activity cards built on students’ own briefs, texts and drafts. Each has 50-minute, 15-minute, one-to-one and mixed-discipline versions, with preparation time. The five that work one-to-one come first, including a talkback consultation on hybrid drafts.
  4. Part Four: Provision designSeven design principles and a four-layer structure: embedded workshops, an AI-aware consultation service, open-access resources and partnership with subject staff. A box sets out the institutional prerequisites: confidentiality, Students’ Union referral, licensed tools, data protection and evaluation.
  5. Part Five: Practitioner developmentA four-stage, 12–24 month development pathway, from grounded tool knowledge to scholarly contribution. Includes four team-level structures and ethical ground rules for practitioners' own AI use.
  6. Part Six: Tensions and objectionsSix open problems stated against the guide's own position, including a thin evidence base, the risk that critique slides into compliance, and limits on practitioner capacity.
